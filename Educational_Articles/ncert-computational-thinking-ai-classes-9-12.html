NCERT’s New Computational Thinking & AI Module for Classes 9–12: What Students Need to Know

You may have seen the news that NCERT is introducing Computational Thinking and Artificial Intelligence (AI) for Classes 9 to 12. Does this mean another subject, another textbook and another board exam? Let’s clear up the confusion in simple language.

1. What exactly has NCERT introduced?

The National Council of Educational Research and Training (NCERT) has developed an introductory module on Computational Thinking (CT) and Artificial Intelligence (AI) for secondary and senior-secondary students in Classes 9 to 12. It aligns with the vision of the National Education Policy (NEP) 2020, which encourages critical thinking, creativity, problem-solving and responsible use of technology.

Think of it as a guided introduction to the way computers solve problems and the way AI tools make predictions or generate answers. The aim is not simply to teach students to use an AI app; it is to help them understand what is happening, check whether an answer makes sense, and recognise when technology can be wrong.

Is it a brand-new subject?

The news headline can sound as though every student must now select a completely separate subject. The safer interpretation is more specific: NCERT has introduced a module, and CBSE's 2026–27 guidance says the Classes 9–12 modules are for internal assessment. Whether and how the module appears in a student's timetable or school assessment plan should be confirmed with the school. Do not confuse this module with an optional vocational/skill subject or assume it replaces Computer Applications, Information Technology or Computer Science.

2. What is Computational Thinking?

Computational Thinking means breaking a problem into manageable steps and designing a clear method to solve it. You do not need to be a programmer to use it.

Everyday example: getting ready for school

You plan the order: wake up, get ready, pack your bag, eat breakfast and leave on time. You organise a task into steps and consider what must happen first. That is similar to algorithmic thinking.

Everyday example: finding a missing notebook

You check the places where you last used it, eliminate places already checked and look for a pattern. You are breaking a larger problem into smaller checks.

Four useful ideas

IdeaMeaning in simple wordsStudent-friendly example
DecompositionBreak a big problem into smaller parts.Divide a school event into venue, invitations, schedule and materials.
Pattern recognitionNotice similarities or repeated trends.Spot the type of question that keeps appearing in a maths worksheet.

These are broad ideas used in computing and problem-solving. The module's approach is practical: understand the idea, try it, discuss it and reflect on what happened.

3. What will students learn about AI?

AI is already part of everyday life: a keyboard suggesting the next word, a music app recommending a song, a map estimating travel time, or a tool answering a question. The module helps students look beyond the screen and ask: How might the system work? What information does it use? When could it make a mistake?

4. Do I need a laptop or coding experience?

No previous AI knowledge is assumed. NCERT says the module is designed for beginners and activities can be conducted with or without digital devices. That means students can discuss a problem, arrange instructions, study examples or analyse a situation even when a computer is not available.

You may encounter demonstrations or digital tools where facilities permit, but don't assume the module requires every student to own a laptop, pay for an AI subscription or learn advanced programming before starting.

Remember: Computational Thinking is not the same thing as coding. Coding is one way to express instructions to a computer; computational thinking is a way to understand and organise a problem. They are related, but they are not identical.

5. Will it affect board exams or marks?

This is probably the question students and parents are most concerned about. The official CBSE guidance for the 2026–27 academic session says that NCERT will provide Computational Thinking and AI modules for Classes 9–12 to be used as modules for internal assessment.

6. Why is NCERT introducing this now?

Students are using AI tools more frequently for explanations, writing, images and searches. Knowing how to open an app is not enough; students also need to know what an AI tool can and cannot do. An answer may contain an invented fact, reflect bias in its data or expose private information if a user shares too much.

That is why responsible use is central to the module. Students are encouraged to verify AI-generated information, consider fairness, protect personal data and remember that human judgement, creativity and ethical responsibility still matter.

7. How should students prepare?

You do not need to panic or buy an expensive course. Start by observing how technology is used around you and practise explaining your reasoning clearly.

  • Break a familiar task into small, ordered steps.
  • Look for repeated patterns in a timetable, a puzzle or a set of numbers.
  • When an AI tool gives an answer, verify important facts with your textbook, teacher or a reliable source.
  • Never share passwords, OTPs, private addresses, school records or personal photographs with an unknown tool.
  • Use AI to ask for another explanation or practice questions, not to submit copied work as your own.
  • Keep notes of what you tried, what worked and what you learned. This habit is useful for reflective, activity-based learning.

Most importantly, stay curious. The purpose is to develop good thinking habits, not to memorise a long list of technical terms.

8. A quick comparison: CT, AI and coding

TermSimple explanationExample
Computational ThinkingA structured way to break down and solve problems.Planning a step-by-step method to find the highest mark in a list.
Artificial IntelligenceComputer systems performing tasks such as recognising patterns, making predictions or generating content.A tool that identifies objects in an image or suggests a reply.
Coding / ProgrammingWriting instructions in a language a computer can run.Writing a small program to calculate an average.

These ideas support one another, but each means something different. A student can practise computational thinking without writing code, and an AI tool can be used without fully understanding how it works—which is exactly why critical thinking and verification are important.

Frequently asked questions

Is Computational Thinking and AI compulsory for all Classes 9–12 students?

NCERT has developed the module for Classes 9–12, and CBSE's 2026–27 guidance refers to its use for internal assessment. For the exact implementation and requirements that apply to you, check your school's official instructions.

Is it a separate subject with a board examination?

The module announcement should not be interpreted as confirmation of a separate board examination for every student. CBSE's guidance describes the Classes 9–12 modules as modules for internal assessment. Ask the school about the assessment process for your class.

Do students need to know Python or another programming language?

No prior knowledge of AI is assumed, and the activities may be completed with or without digital devices. The introductory module is not described as requiring students to arrive with programming experience.

Will it replace Computer Science, Information Technology or Computer Applications?

Do not assume that it replaces any existing subject. Follow the subject list and academic instructions officially issued for your class.

What is the biggest skill students should take away?

Think step by step, question results that seem suspicious, protect personal information and verify important claims instead of accepting an AI answer automatically.
