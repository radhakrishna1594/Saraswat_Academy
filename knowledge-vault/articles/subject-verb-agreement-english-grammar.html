Subject–Verb Agreement: Rules and Examples

A sentence sounds correct when its subject and verb agree. Learn when to use is or are, has or have, and singular or plural verb forms with clear rules and practice.

1. What is subject–verb agreement?

Subject–verb agreement means that the verb matches its subject in number and person. A singular subject usually takes a singular verb, while a plural subject usually takes a plural verb.

SubjectCorrect verbExample
He / She / Itis, has, worksShe works hard.
Iam, have, workI am ready.
You / We / Theyare, have, workThey are ready.
Plural nounare, have, workThe players practise daily.

In the simple present tense, we usually add -s or -es to the verb with he, she, it or a singular noun: Ravi reads. With I, you, we, they and plural nouns, we use the base form: They read.

2. Rule 1: A singular subject takes a singular verb

  • The child plays in the garden.
  • My brother likes science.
  • The bus arrives at eight.

Do not be confused by a long subject. Find the main subject and match the verb to it.

Correct: The box of old books is heavy. The subject is box, not books.

3. Rule 2: A plural subject takes a plural verb

  • The children play outside.
  • My friends like music.
  • These buses arrive on time.

Remember that present-tense verbs usually do not take -s with plural subjects: The students study, not The students studies.

4. Rule 3: Two subjects joined by “and”

Two subjects joined by and usually take a plural verb.

  • Riya and Meena are classmates.
  • A pen and a pencil lie on the desk.

When two words describe one person or one combined idea, a singular verb may be used depending on the meaning.

  • Bread and butter is my usual breakfast. (One combined meal or item.)

5. Rule 4: “Or”, “nor” and “either…or”

When subjects are joined by or, nor, either…or or neither…nor, the verb usually agrees with the subject closest to it.

  • Either the teacher or the students are coming.
  • Either the students or the teacher is coming.
  • Neither Rohan nor his friends have arrived.

For smoother sentences, especially when the subjects differ in number, you can often rewrite the sentence to avoid an awkward construction.

6. Rule 5: Words between the subject and verb

Words or phrases between the subject and verb do not usually change the agreement.

  • The bouquet of roses smells lovely.
  • The captain, along with the players, is present.
  • The students in this classroom are attentive.

Expressions such as along with, as well as and together with do not normally turn a singular subject into a plural one.

7. Rule 6: Indefinite pronouns

Words such as everyone, everybody, someone, somebody, anyone, each and everybody are grammatically singular in standard subject–verb agreement.

  • Everyone is welcome.
  • Each of the players has a uniform.
  • Someone knows the answer.

Both, few, many and several usually take plural verbs.

  • Several were absent.
  • Many have completed the task.

8. Rule 7: “There is” and “there are”

In sentences beginning with there, the verb agrees with the noun that follows it.

  • There is a book on the table.
  • There are three books on the table.
  • There has been a delay.
  • There have been several delays.

9. Rule 8: Uncountable nouns and collective nouns

Uncountable nouns such as information, furniture, advice and equipment generally take singular verbs.

  • The information is useful.
  • This furniture looks new.
  • Your advice has helped me.

Collective nouns such as team, family and committee can take a singular verb when the group is viewed as one unit. In British English, a plural verb may also be used when members are considered individually. Follow the style expected in your course or examination.

  • The team is ready for the match. (The team as one unit.)
  • The family are arguing among themselves. (Members considered individually; common in British English.)

10. Rule 9: Amounts, distances and time periods

When an amount, distance or period of time is considered one total unit, it usually takes a singular verb.

  • Five kilometres is a long walk for me.
  • Ten minutes is enough.
  • One hundred rupees is the entry fee.

If the sentence focuses on separate items or units, a plural verb may be appropriate: Ten minutes have passed.

11. Common mistakes

IncorrectCorrectWhy?

12. Practice exercises

Choose the correct verb or correct the sentence. Open each answer after trying it yourself.

  1. My sister (walk / walks) to school every day.
    Show answer

    walks — “My sister” is singular.

  2. The teachers (is / are) in the staff room.
    Show answer

    are — “Teachers” is plural.

  3. Each of these questions (has / have) an answer.
    Show answer

    has — “Each” is singular.

  4. The basket of mangoes (is / are) on the table.
    Show answer

    is — The main subject is “basket”.

  5. Either the principal or the teachers (is / are) attending.
    Show answer

    are — The nearer subject, “teachers”, is plural.

  6. There (is / are) two notebooks in my bag.
    Show answer

    Show answer

  8. Correct the sentence: The quality of these apples are good.
    Show answer

    The quality of these apples is good. The subject is “quality”.

  9. Ten minutes (is / are) enough to finish this task.
    Show answer

  10. Correct the sentence: She don't like coffee.
    Show answer

13. Quick revision

  • First identify the main subject; do not be distracted by extra words.
  • A singular subject usually takes a singular verb; a plural subject usually takes a plural verb.
  • Subjects joined by and usually take a plural verb.
  • With either…or and neither…nor, the verb commonly agrees with the nearer subject.
  • Each, everyone and someone are grammatically singular.
  • In “there is/are” sentences, check the noun that follows the verb.
