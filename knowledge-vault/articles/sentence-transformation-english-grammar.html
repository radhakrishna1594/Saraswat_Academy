Sentence Transformation: Simple, Compound and Complex Forms

Sentence transformation means expressing the same idea in a different grammatical form. Learn how to change simple, compound and complex sentences while keeping the original meaning clear.

1. What Is Sentence Transformation?

Sentence transformation is the process of rewriting a sentence in another form without changing its essential meaning. In school grammar, a common task is to change a simple sentence into a compound or complex sentence, or to change one of these forms into another.

Example: “Despite the rain, we continued the match.” can be rewritten as “Although it was raining, we continued the match.” The structure changes, but the central idea remains the same.

Always preserve the meaning, tense, time relationship and logical connection between ideas. Do not add information that the original sentence does not contain.

2. Revise the Three Sentence Structures

TypeStructureExample
SimpleOne independent clause; it may contain a phrase or a non-finite verb.Being tired, Maya went to bed early.
CompoundTwo or more independent clauses joined appropriately.Maya was tired, so she went to bed early.
ComplexOne independent clause and at least one dependent clause.Because Maya was tired, she went to bed early.

A clause contains a subject and a verb. An independent clause can stand alone as a complete sentence; a dependent clause relies on another clause to complete its meaning.

3. Simple to Compound Sentences

A simple sentence can often be changed into a compound sentence by expanding a phrase into an independent clause and joining the ideas with a suitable coordinating conjunction, such as and, but, so, or, yet.

  • Simple: In spite of his illness, Ravi attended class.
  • Compound: Ravi was ill, but he attended class.
  • Simple: Being hungry, the children ate quickly.
  • Compound: The children were hungry, so they ate quickly.
  • Simple: Work hard to succeed.
  • Compound: Work hard, and you will succeed.

Tip: Choose the conjunction according to the relationship: and adds information, but shows contrast, and so introduces a result.

4. Compound to Simple Sentences

To make a compound sentence simple, one clause can sometimes be changed into a phrase, a participial construction, or an infinitive phrase. Keep the relationship between the ideas intact.

  • Compound: He finished his work, and he went home.
  • Simple: Having finished his work, he went home.
  • Compound: She wanted to improve, so she practised daily.
  • Simple: She practised daily to improve.
  • Compound: The road was narrow, but the bus passed safely.
  • Simple: Despite the narrow road, the bus passed safely.

Not every compound sentence has a natural simple equivalent. Avoid awkward phrases or changes that distort the original meaning.

5. Simple to Complex Sentences

A simple sentence may be transformed into a complex sentence by replacing a phrase with a dependent clause. Common links include although, because, when, if, who, which and that.

  • Simple: In spite of his hard work, he failed.
  • Complex: Although he worked hard, he failed.
  • Simple: I know the answer to the question.
  • Complex: I know what the answer to the question is.
  • Simple: On reaching the station, she called me.
  • Complex: When she reached the station, she called me.

Check that the new dependent clause expresses the same time, reason, condition or contrast as the original phrase.

6. Complex to Simple Sentences

To change a complex sentence into a simple one, a dependent clause may be reduced to a phrase or a non-finite construction when the subject and meaning allow it.

  • Complex: When he saw the police officer, the thief ran away.
  • Simple: Seeing the police officer, the thief ran away.
  • Complex: Because she was unwell, she stayed at home.
  • Simple: Being unwell, she stayed at home.
  • Complex: He works hard so that he may support his family.
  • Simple: He works hard to support his family.

Be careful with participial phrases: the person performing the action should normally be the subject of the main clause. For example, “Walking down the road, the rain began” is faulty because it suggests that the rain was walking.

7. Compound to Complex Sentences

A compound sentence can become complex when one independent clause is made dependent using a subordinating conjunction or a relative word.

  • Compound: It was raining, so we stayed indoors.
  • Complex: Because it was raining, we stayed indoors.
  • Compound: He is poor, but he is honest.
  • Complex: Although he is poor, he is honest.
  • Compound: Follow the instructions, or you may make a mistake.
  • Complex: If you do not follow the instructions, you may make a mistake.

The conjunction can change when the logical relationship calls for it. Use because for a reason, although for contrast and if for a condition.

8. Complex to Compound Sentences

To transform a complex sentence into a compound one, the dependent clause can sometimes be rewritten as an independent clause. Join the clauses with a coordinating conjunction that preserves the relationship.

  • Complex: Although he was tired, he completed the task.
  • Compound: He was tired, but he completed the task.
  • Complex: Since the shop was closed, we returned home.
  • Compound: The shop was closed, so we returned home.
  • Complex: If you hurry, you will catch the bus.
  • Compound: Hurry, or you will miss the bus.

The last example expresses a warning, so the compound form uses a natural equivalent rather than copying the words mechanically.

9. Useful Connectors and Their Functions

MeaningCommon connectorExample
Reasonbecause, sinceBecause it was late, we left.
Contrastalthough, though, but, yetAlthough it was late, we continued.
Resultso, thereforeIt was late, so we left.
Timewhen, after, before, as soon asWhen the bell rang, we left.
Conditionif, unlessIf you practise, you will improve.
Purposeso that, to, in order toShe saved money to buy a book.

These connectors are not always interchangeable. Select one that matches the meaning rather than choosing one only because it fits the sentence pattern.

10. A Reliable Method for Transformation

  1. Read the original sentence and identify its main idea.
  2. Identify the sentence type and locate its clauses or phrases.
  3. Determine the logical link: addition, contrast, reason, result, time, purpose or condition.
  4. Choose a suitable conjunction or phrase to build the new structure.
  5. Check subjects, verb forms, tense and punctuation.
  6. Read both versions and confirm that they communicate the same idea.

In examinations, write the complete transformed sentence. Do not leave out a necessary subject, verb or part of the original meaning.

11. Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Changing the meaning: “Although” shows contrast; “because” gives a reason. They are not substitutes for each other.
  • Using a comma splice: Two independent clauses should not normally be joined by a comma alone. Use a conjunction or appropriate punctuation.
  • Creating a dangling participle: The implied subject of a phrase such as “After finishing the work” must be clear.
  • Using the wrong verb form: “Having finished” and “to finish” express different relationships.
  • Adding unnecessary words: Keep the transformed sentence natural and concise.
  • Forgetting punctuation: A dependent clause at the beginning is usually followed by a comma; punctuation may differ when it comes at the end.

13. Practice Exercise B: Transform the Sentences

Rewrite each sentence as directed. Preserve its meaning as closely as possible.

  1. Although he was poor, he was generous. (Change into compound.)
  2. She hurried to catch the train. (Change into complex.)
  3. It was very cold, so we stayed inside. (Change into complex.)
  4. When the teacher entered, the class became silent. (Change into simple.)
  5. Work regularly, or you will fall behind. (Change into complex.)
Show suggested answers

  2. She hurried so that she could catch the train.
  3. Because it was very cold, we stayed inside.
  4. On the teacher's entering, the class became silent. (A more natural alternative may be used where appropriate.)
  5. If you do not work regularly, you will fall behind.

14. Practice Exercise C: Correct the Error

Each sentence has a structural or logical problem. Rewrite it correctly.

  1. Although she was tired, but she continued working.
  2. Because he missed the bus, so he arrived late.
  3. Walking to school, the rain started heavily.
  4. He was ill, he went to the doctor.
Show suggested corrections
  1. Although she was tired, she continued working. Or: She was tired, but she continued working.
  2. Because he missed the bus, he arrived late. Or: He missed the bus, so he arrived late.
  3. While I was walking to school, it started raining heavily. (The subject must be the person walking.)
  4. He was ill, so he went to the doctor. Or: Because he was ill, he went to the doctor.

15. Quick Revision

  • A simple sentence has one independent clause; a compound sentence joins independent clauses; a complex sentence includes a dependent clause.
  • Sentence transformation changes structure, not the essential meaning.
  • Use coordinating conjunctions such as and, but, so and or to join ideas in compound sentences.
  • Use subordinating words such as because, although, when and if to introduce dependent clauses.
  • Phrases and non-finite verbs can help convert clauses into simple-sentence structures.
  • Always check meaning, grammar, subject reference and punctuation.

The best way to improve is to practise transformations in pairs. First identify the relationship between the ideas, then choose a structure that expresses that relationship naturally.
