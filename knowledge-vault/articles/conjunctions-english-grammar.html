Conjunctions in English Grammar: Types and Examples

Read these sentences: “Ravi likes tea and coffee.” “I wanted to play, but it was raining.” “We stayed indoors because it was raining.” The highlighted words connect words or ideas. They are called conjunctions.

1. What is a conjunction?

A conjunction is a word that joins words, phrases or clauses. It helps show how the ideas are related—for example, addition, contrast, choice, reason, time or condition.

ConnectionExampleWhat it shows
WordsI bought apples and oranges.Addition
Ideas in contrastHe is young, but he is wise.Contrast
ChoiceWould you like tea or coffee?Choice
ReasonWe stayed home because it rained.Reason

2. Coordinating conjunctions

Coordinating conjunctions join words, phrases or clauses of equal grammatical importance. A useful memory aid is FANBOYS.

ConjunctionUseExample
ForReason or explanation; somewhat formalWe went inside, for the wind was getting stronger.
AndAdditionShe sings and dances.
NorAdds a negative ideaHe did not call, nor did he send a message.
ButContrastI was tired, but I finished my work.
OrChoice or alternativeHurry up, or you will miss the bus.
YetUnexpected contrastThe task was difficult, yet she completed it.
SoResultIt was late, so we went home.

Punctuation tip: When a coordinating conjunction joins two complete sentences, a comma is commonly placed before it: “I called her, but she did not answer.” A comma is not usually needed when joining two simple words: “tea and coffee.”

3. Subordinating conjunctions

Subordinating conjunctions introduce a dependent clause—a group of words that cannot usually stand alone as a complete sentence in that use. The clause gives information about reason, time, condition, contrast or purpose.

RelationshipCommon conjunctionsExample
Reasonbecause, since, asI wore a jacket because it was cold.
Timewhen, while, before, after, untilCall me when you arrive.
Conditionif, unless, provided thatYou will improve if you practise.
Contrast or concessionalthough, though, even thoughAlthough it rained, we continued.
Purposeso that, in order thatSpeak clearly so that everyone can hear.
Comparisonthan, as...asShe is taller than I expected.

A dependent clause can come first or second. When it comes first, a comma is commonly used after it: “Although it was late, we continued studying.” When it comes after the main clause, the comma is often unnecessary: “We continued studying although it was late.”

4. Correlative conjunctions

Correlative conjunctions work in pairs. They connect balanced parts of a sentence, so try to keep the grammar on both sides parallel.

PairExample
either...orYou can either walk or take the bus.
neither...norNeither Riya nor Meena was absent.
both...andBoth my brother and my sister enjoy reading.
not only...but alsoHe is not only helpful but also patient.
whether...orI cannot decide whether to stay or leave.

Parallel structure: “She likes both reading and swimming” sounds balanced. Avoid mixing forms unnecessarily, such as “She likes both reading and to swim.”

5. Choosing the right conjunction

Meaning you wantChooseExample
Add one more ideaandWe bought fruit and vegetables.
Show a contrastbut, yet, althoughIt was cold, but we went out.
Give a reasonbecause, sinceI rested because I was tired.
Show a resultsoI was tired, so I rested.
Give a choiceor, either...orYou may choose tea or juice.
Give a conditionif, unlessYou will miss the bus unless you hurry.

Compare because and so: “We stayed inside because it rained” gives the reason. “It rained, so we stayed inside” gives the result. In standard writing, avoid using both together in the same simple structure: “Because it rained, so we stayed inside.”

6. Common mistakes

IncorrectBetter sentenceReason
Although he was tired, but he worked.Although he was tired, he worked.Do not normally use although and but together for the same contrast.
Because it was raining, so we stayed home.Because it was raining, we stayed home.Because already introduces the reason.
She is both smart as well as kind.She is both smart and kind.Use the pair both...and.
Either you apologise nor leave.Either you apologise or leave.Use either...or.
I like tea, coffee.I like tea and coffee.Use a conjunction to join the items.

7. Practice questions

  1. Fill in the blank: I wanted to go out, ______ it was raining. (but / because)
  2. Choose the conjunction of reason: We stayed home ______ we were unwell. (because / or)
  3. Complete the pair: ______ you study regularly, you will improve. (If / Yet)
  4. Join the sentences using and: “Aman reads. Aman writes.”
  5. Correct the sentence: “Although she was tired, but she finished her homework.”
  6. Complete the pair: You can either call me ______ send a message.
  7. Identify the conjunction: “Wait here until I return.”
  8. Choose the correct word: It was getting late, ______ we left. (so / because)
Show answers and explanations
  1. but — it shows contrast.
  2. because — it gives the reason.
  3. If — it introduces a condition.
  4. “Aman reads and writes.”
  5. “Although she was tired, she finished her homework.” Alternatively: “She was tired, but she finished her homework.”
  6. or — either...or gives a choice.
  7. until — it introduces a time clause.
  8. so — it introduces the result.
