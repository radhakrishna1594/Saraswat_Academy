Conditional Sentences: Zero, First, Second and Third Conditionals

Conditional sentences describe what happens, may happen, could happen, or might have happened under a particular condition. Learn the four main patterns and how to use them accurately.

1. What Is a Conditional Sentence?

A conditional sentence describes a condition and its result. It often contains an if-clause and a main clause.

  • If you heat ice, it melts.
  • If it rains tomorrow, we will stay indoors.
  • If I had more time, I would learn another language.

The part beginning with if gives the condition. The other part gives the result. The order can change: We will stay indoors if it rains tomorrow.

2. Zero Conditional

Use the zero conditional for general truths, scientific facts, habits and results that normally follow a condition.

Structure: If + simple present, simple present.

  • If you heat water to 100°C at standard atmospheric pressure, it boils.
  • If plants do not get enough light, they grow poorly.
  • If I drink coffee late at night, I find it difficult to sleep.

The result is presented as generally true, usual or predictable. The word when can sometimes replace if when the condition is expected to occur.

3. First Conditional

Use the first conditional for a real or possible future situation and its likely result.

Structure: If + simple present, will + base verb.

  • If you study regularly, you will improve.
  • If the weather is good tomorrow, we will play outside.
  • If she arrives early, we can start the meeting.

The main clause does not always need will. Depending on the meaning, it may use can, may, might, should or an imperative.

  • If you finish your work, you may leave.
  • If you see Rohan, tell him to call me.

In the usual first conditional pattern, do not put will in the if-clause: write If it rains, we will stay home, not If it will rain, we will stay home.

4. Second Conditional

Use the second conditional for an imaginary, unlikely or hypothetical present or future situation.

Structure: If + simple past, would + base verb.

  • If I had a million rupees, I would help my family.
  • If she lived closer, we would meet more often.
  • If I were you, I would apologise.

The past form here usually signals distance from reality, not past time. In formal or traditional grammar, were is commonly used with all subjects in hypothetical expressions such as If I were you. In everyday speech, was is also heard in some contexts.

5. Third Conditional

Use the third conditional to imagine a different result for a past situation that did not happen as described.

Structure: If + past perfect, would have + past participle (V3).

  • If I had left earlier, I would have caught the bus.
  • If they had practised more, they might have won the match.
  • If she had known about the meeting, she would have attended.

This form is often used to express regret, criticism or an imagined alternative past. It does not change what actually happened.

7. Using Unless

Unless usually means if not. It introduces a condition that must be met to avoid the result.

  • If you do not hurry, you will miss the bus.
  • Unless you hurry, you will miss the bus.
  • If she does not study, she may not pass.
  • Unless she studies, she may not pass.

Do not normally add another negative to the clause after unless when you mean “if not”. For example, Unless you do not hurry usually changes or confuses the intended meaning.

8. Other Words Used in Conditionals

  • As long as / provided that: express a requirement. You can borrow the book as long as you return it tomorrow.
  • Even if: says the result will remain the same despite a condition. Even if it rains, we will go.
  • In case: describes a precaution for a possible situation. Take an umbrella in case it rains.

These expressions are related to conditions, but they do not always follow exactly the same pattern or meaning as if.

9. Mixed Conditionals

A mixed conditional combines a past condition with a present result, or a present condition with a past result. The tense combination depends on the intended meaning.

Past condition, present result

If I had gone to bed earlier, I would not be so tired now.

The past action did not happen, and its imagined effect is present.

Present condition, past result

If she were more organised, she would not have missed the appointment yesterday.

The sentence connects an imagined general characteristic with a past result.

10. Punctuation and Word Order

  • When the if-clause comes first, use a comma between it and the main clause: If you need help, call me.
  • When the main clause comes first, a comma is usually unnecessary: Call me if you need help.
  • In common conditional patterns, use present forms in the if-clause for a real future possibility: If she comes, we will begin.
  • Use the verb forms that match the intended time and meaning. Do not choose a conditional type based only on the word if.

11. Common Mistakes

  • Incorrect: If it will rain, we will cancel the match. Usual form: If it rains, we will cancel the match.
  • Incorrect: If I would have money, I would travel. Correct: If I had money, I would travel.
  • Incorrect: If she had studied, she would pass the exam yesterday. Correct third conditional: If she had studied, she would have passed the exam.
  • Incorrect: Unless you do not practise, you will not improve. Clearer intended form: If you do not practise, you will not improve. / Unless you practise, you will not improve.

12. Practice Exercise A: Identify the Conditional

Identify each sentence as zero, first, second, third or mixed conditional.

  1. If you freeze water, it turns into ice.
  2. If I see Neha, I will tell her.
  3. If he had more free time, he would learn guitar.
  4. If we had booked earlier, we would have paid less.
  5. If I had taken the job, I would live in Delhi now.
Show answers
  1. Zero conditional — a general fact.
  2. First conditional — a real future possibility.
  3. Second conditional — an imaginary present situation.
  4. Third conditional — an imagined alternative past.
  5. Mixed conditional — a past condition with a present result.

15. Quick Revision

  • Zero: facts, habits and usual results.
  • First: real or possible future situations.
  • Second: imaginary or unlikely present and future situations.
  • Third: imaginary alternatives to past events.
  • Mixed: a condition and result referring to different times.
  • Unless: usually means “if not”.

Start by asking whether the sentence describes a general truth, a real future possibility, an imaginary situation or an alternative past. That will help you select the right conditional structure.
