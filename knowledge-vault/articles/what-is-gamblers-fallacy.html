What Is Gambler’s Fallacy?

The gambler’s fallacy is the mistaken belief that if one random outcome has happened many times in a row, the opposite outcome must now be more likely.

It is a common mistake in probability. Understanding it helps us judge random events more clearly instead of assuming that chance must “balance itself out” on the very next try.

Meaning of Gambler’s Fallacy

Many people think that if something random happens many times in a row—like flipping a coin and getting heads six times—then the opposite outcome, tails, is more likely to happen next. But a fair coin does not keep track of what happened before. Every flip is a new trial, and the chance of heads or tails remains the same. The coin has no memory, so past flips do not change the next flip’s probability. This common misunderstanding is called the gambler’s fallacy.

The key idea is that a streak may feel unusual, but it does not force the next result to be the opposite.

Example: A Fair Coin Lands on Heads Six Times

Imagine that you flip a fair coin and it comes up heads six times in a row. You might feel that tails is “due” because it has not appeared for a while. However, the probability of getting tails on the next flip is still 50% or 1/2, exactly the same as before.

The coin has no memory of past flips. If the coin is fair and each flip is independent, the previous six heads do not make tails more likely on the seventh flip.

Why Does This Happen?

People often expect random results to look balanced over a short period. After seeing a long streak, we may think the other result must appear soon to make things even. But probability does not promise an immediate correction.

  • Randomness can create streaks. Runs of the same result can occur by chance.
  • Each independent trial starts afresh. Earlier outcomes do not change the probability of the next one.
  • Long-run balance is not a next-turn guarantee. Over many flips, the proportion of heads may tend towards 50%, but that does not mean tails must come next.

Probability Behind the Example

For a fair coin, the two possible outcomes on one flip are equally likely:

P(Heads) = 1/2 = 0.5 = 50%

P(Tails) = 1/2 = 0.5 = 50%

Assuming independent flips, the probability of getting tails on the next flip remains 1/2, regardless of the previous sequence. The probability of getting six heads in six specified flips is ((1/2)^6 = 1/64). That describes the chance of that six-flip sequence before it happens; it does not change the probability of the next flip after the sequence has occurred.

Everyday Examples of Gambler’s Fallacy

1. Tossing a coin

After heads appears several times, someone predicts that tails is more likely next. For a fair coin, that prediction is the fallacy.

2. Roulette or other games of chance

If a colour has appeared repeatedly in independent rounds, a player may believe the other colour must be next. A streak alone does not make that outcome more likely. This belief can lead to risky decisions and losses.

3. Lottery numbers

A person may avoid a number because it has appeared recently, or choose a number because it has not appeared for a long time. In a fair, independent lottery draw, past draws do not make a particular eligible number “due”.

4. Random number simulations

When testing a program that generates random values, a short run of repeated results does not automatically mean the next value must be different. A longer test and appropriate statistical checks are needed to assess whether a generator behaves as expected.

Gambler’s Fallacy vs. Real Patterns

The gambler’s fallacy applies when outcomes are independent and the underlying probabilities stay the same. It should not be confused with situations where the process genuinely changes or outcomes are dependent.

  • Independent trials: A fair coin flip does not affect the next flip.
  • Dependent events: Drawing cards without replacement changes what remains in the deck, so probabilities can change after each draw.
  • Changing conditions: If a machine, process, or system is biased or has changed, earlier observations may be useful evidence—but a streak alone is not proof.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Thinking that the opposite result must occur after a streak.
  • Confusing long-run averages with a guarantee about the next trial.
  • Assuming every sequence that looks unusual is impossible or unfair.
  • Using past results to predict an independent event without evidence that its probability has changed.

Quick Revision

  • Definition: The gambler’s fallacy is the mistaken belief that a random outcome becomes more likely because it has not happened recently.
  • Coin example: After six heads, the chance of tails on the next fair coin flip is still 1/2.
  • Reason: Independent events do not remember earlier outcomes.
  • Remember: A result can be “overdue” in our minds without being more likely mathematically.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the gambler’s fallacy always wrong?

It is wrong when used to predict independent events with unchanged probabilities, such as flips of a fair coin. In dependent situations, probabilities may change, so the conditions must be checked.

After six heads, what is the chance of tails next?

For a fair coin with independent flips, the chance is 1/2, or 50%.

Does probability balance itself after a streak?

Not necessarily in the next few trials. Over a large number of independent flips, the proportion of heads may tend towards one-half, but streaks can continue and no specific next result is guaranteed.

Why is it called the gambler’s fallacy?

The error is often associated with games of chance, where people mistakenly believe that a run of results makes the opposite result more likely in the next independent round.
