What Is Trigonometry? Complete Notes, Ratios, Identities and Real-Life Uses

Trigonometry connects the angles of a triangle with the lengths of its sides. It helps us calculate heights, distances, slopes, directions and repeating patterns that are difficult to measure directly.

This guide explains the six ratios, standard-angle table, identities, real-world applications and solved exam-style questions.

How to study: learn the side names first, memorise the six ratios, practise the standard-angle table, and then use identities to simplify or prove expressions. Core ratios and heights-and-distances are important in Class 10; radians, unit-circle definitions and compound-angle formulae are studied further in senior-secondary mathematics. 1. What Is Trigonometry?

Trigonometry is the branch of mathematics that studies relationships between angles and sides of triangles. In elementary geometry, it mainly uses ratios in right-angled triangles. At higher levels, it also studies rotations, waves and periodic phenomena.

For a fixed acute angle, the ratios of corresponding sides remain constant in similar triangles. Therefore, if an angle and one side are known, a suitable trigonometric ratio can help calculate an unknown side.

Uses in real life

  • Finding the height of a building, tree, tower or mountain.
  • Calculating distances across a river or between inaccessible points.
  • Surveying, navigation, mapping and astronomy.
  • Designing roofs, ramps, bridges and other structures.
  • Physics: forces, sound, light, waves and circular motion.
  • Computer graphics, animation, robotics, games and satellite positioning.

2. Understand the Right-Angled Triangle

  • Hypotenuse (H): side opposite the right angle; always the longest side.
  • Opposite (O): side directly opposite the chosen reference angle.
  • Adjacent (A): side next to the chosen angle, other than the hypotenuse.
Remember: “opposite” and “adjacent” depend on the reference angle. The hypotenuse is always opposite 90°.

3. All Six Trigonometric Ratios

For an acute angle θ in a right-angled triangle:

sin θ = Opposite / Hypotenuse = O/H

cos θ = Adjacent / Hypotenuse = A/H

tan θ = Opposite / Adjacent = O/A

cosec θ = Hypotenuse / Opposite = H/O

sec θ = Hypotenuse / Adjacent = H/A

cot θ = Adjacent / Opposite = A/O

Remember: SOH–CAH–TOA

SOH: Sine = Opposite/Hypotenuse.

CAH: Cosine = Adjacent/Hypotenuse.

TOA: Tangent = Opposite/Adjacent.

Example: a 3–4–5 triangle

If opposite = 3, adjacent = 4 and hypotenuse = 5, then sin θ = 3/5, cos θ = 4/5, tan θ = 3/4, cosec θ = 5/3, sec θ = 5/4 and cot θ = 4/3.

Pattern for sine and cosine

For sine, use √0/2, √1/2, √2/2, √3/2, √4/2 in order.

Thus sin θ = 0, 1/2, 1/√2, √3/2, 1. The cosine row is the same list in reverse order.

A ratio with denominator zero is undefined. For example, tan 90° = sin 90°/cos 90° = 1/0 is undefined, whereas cot 90° = 0.

5. Ratios of Complementary Angles

Complementary angles add up to 90°. If A + B = 90°, then:

sin (90° − θ) = cos θ

cos (90° − θ) = sin θ

tan (90° − θ) = cot θ

cot (90° − θ) = tan θ

sec (90° − θ) = cosec θ

cosec (90° − θ) = sec θ

For example, sin 30° = cos 60° = 1/2, and tan 30° = cot 60° = 1/√3.

7. Trigonometric Identities

A trigonometric identity is an equality involving trigonometric functions that is true for every angle in the common domain where both sides are defined. Identities are used to simplify expressions, calculate unknown ratios and prove statements.

7.1 Reciprocal identities

cosec θ = 1/sin θ;   sin θ = 1/cosec θ

sec θ = 1/cos θ;   cos θ = 1/sec θ

cot θ = 1/tan θ;   tan θ = 1/cot θ

7.2 Quotient identities

tan θ = sin θ / cos θ

cot θ = cos θ / sin θ

7.3 Fundamental Pythagorean identities

sin² θ + cos² θ = 1

1 + tan² θ = sec² θ

1 + cot² θ = cosec² θ

In sin² θ, the exponent applies to the value of sine: sin² θ means (sin θ)², not sin(θ²).

7.4 Rearranged forms

sin² θ = 1 − cos² θ

cos² θ = 1 − sin² θ

tan² θ = sec² θ − 1

sec² θ − tan² θ = 1

cot² θ = cosec² θ − 1

cosec² θ − cot² θ = 1

7.5 Complementary-angle identities

sin (90° − θ) = cos θ

cos (90° − θ) = sin θ

tan (90° − θ) = cot θ

cot (90° − θ) = tan θ

sec (90° − θ) = cosec θ

cosec (90° − θ) = sec θ

7.6 Sum and difference formulae (higher-level)

sin (A + B) = sin A cos B + cos A sin B

sin (A − B) = sin A cos B − cos A sin B

cos (A + B) = cos A cos B − sin A sin B

cos (A − B) = cos A cos B + sin A sin B

tan (A + B) = (tan A + tan B)/(1 − tan A tan B)

tan (A − B) = (tan A − tan B)/(1 + tan A tan B)

The tangent formulae require all terms to be defined and the displayed denominators to be non-zero.

7.7 Double-angle formulae

sin 2A = 2 sin A cos A

cos 2A = cos² A − sin² A

cos 2A = 1 − 2 sin² A = 2 cos² A − 1

tan 2A = 2 tan A / (1 − tan² A)

7.8 Half-angle formulae

sin² (A/2) = (1 − cos A)/2

cos² (A/2) = (1 + cos A)/2

tan² (A/2) = (1 − cos A)/(1 + cos A)

When taking square roots, choose the positive or negative sign according to the quadrant of A/2.

7.9 Product-to-sum and sum-to-product formulae

2 sin A cos B = sin (A + B) + sin (A − B)

2 cos A sin B = sin (A + B) − sin (A − B)

2 cos A cos B = cos (A + B) + cos (A − B)

2 sin A sin B = cos (A − B) − cos (A + B)

sin A + sin B = 2 sin ((A + B)/2) cos ((A − B)/2)

sin A − sin B = 2 cos ((A + B)/2) sin ((A − B)/2)

cos A + cos B = 2 cos ((A + B)/2) cos ((A − B)/2)

cos A − cos B = −2 sin ((A + B)/2) sin ((A − B)/2)

What to learn first: for Class 10, prioritise the three Pythagorean identities, reciprocal and quotient relations, and complementary-angle relations as prescribed. In Class 11–12, also study radians, unit-circle signs, sum/difference, double-angle and half-angle formulae according to the current syllabus.

8. Degrees and Radians

Angles may be measured in degrees or radians. A full revolution is 360° or 2π radians, so 180° = π radians.

Degrees to radians: θ° × π/180

Radians to degrees: θ × 180/π

Degrees0°30°45°60°90°180°270°360°
Radians0π/6π/4π/3π/2π3π/22π

Example: convert 150° into radians

150 × π/180 = 5π/6 radians.

Example: convert 2π/3 radians into degrees

(2π/3) × (180/π) = 120°.

8.1 The unit-circle idea

On a unit circle of radius 1 centred at the origin, the point corresponding to angle θ has coordinates (cos θ, sin θ). This explains why cosine is the horizontal coordinate, sine is the vertical coordinate, and sin² θ + cos² θ = 1.

9. Uses of Trigonometry in Real Life

9.1 Finding the height of a building or tree

If horizontal distance is d and angle of elevation is θ, ignoring observer height:

tan θ = height/d   ⇒   height = d tan θ

9.2 Elevation and depression

  • Angle of elevation: angle between the horizontal and line of sight when looking upward.
  • Angle of depression: angle between the horizontal and line of sight when looking downward.

When the horizontal lines are parallel, the angle of depression at the top and the angle of elevation at the bottom are equal as alternate interior angles.

9.3 Other practical uses

Surveying: estimate a tower's height or the width of a river using measured baselines and angles.

Construction: calculate roof slopes, ramp gradients, stairs and bridge components. Engineering designs also require safety factors and material checks.

Navigation and astronomy: describe bearings, satellite geometry and positions of celestial objects.

Physics and technology: analyse forces, sound waves, rotating machinery, animation, robotics and movement in computer graphics.

10. Solved Examples

Example 1 · Ratios

Opposite = 5 cm and hypotenuse = 13 cm. Find sin θ and cos θ.

Adjacent² = 13² − 5² = 144, so adjacent = 12 cm. Therefore sin θ = 5/13 and cos θ = 12/13.

Example 2 · Standard angles

Evaluate 2 sin 30° + cos 60°.

= 2(1/2) + 1/2 = 1 + 1/2 = 3/2.

Example 3 · Identity

Simplify (1 − cos² θ)/sin² θ.

Since 1 − cos² θ = sin² θ, the expression equals 1, wherever defined.

Example 4 · Height and distance

A student stands 20 m from a pole. The angle of elevation is 45°. Find the pole's height, ignoring eye height.

tan 45° = h/20. Since tan 45° = 1, h = 20 m.

Example 5 · Height and distance

From 10 m away, the angle of elevation to a tower is 60°. Find its height.

tan 60° = h/10, so h = 10√3 m ≈ 17.32 m.

Example 6 · Find a ratio

If sin θ = 3/5 and θ is acute, find cos θ and tan θ.

Opposite = 3, hypotenuse = 5, so adjacent = 4. Hence cos θ = 4/5 and tan θ = 3/4.

Example 7 · Prove an identity

Prove sec² θ − tan² θ = 1.

Divide sin² θ + cos² θ = 1 by cos² θ: tan² θ + 1 = sec² θ. Rearranging gives the result.

11. Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing opposite and adjacent: identify them relative to the marked angle.
  • Forgetting that the hypotenuse is always opposite the right angle.
  • Mixing up sine and cosine values. The cosine row reverses the sine row for the standard angles.
  • Writing tan 90° = 0. It is undefined; cot 90° = 0.
  • Confusing reciprocal ratios with negative values: cosec θ = 1/sin θ.
  • Dividing an identity by an expression that may be zero without checking its domain.
  • Forgetting observer's eye height if a height-and-distance question includes it.

12. Exam Practice Questions

Try each question before revealing the answer. These are original practice questions based on common school-level concepts, not verbatim copies of a specific board paper.

1 mark

Q1. Write tan θ in terms of sin θ and cos θ.

Show answer

tan θ = sin θ/cos θ, where cos θ ≠ 0.

1 mark

Q2. Find cos 60°.

Show answer

1/2.

2 marks

Q3. If tan θ = 3/4 for an acute angle, find sin θ.

Show answer

Opposite = 3, adjacent = 4, hypotenuse = 5. Therefore sin θ = 3/5.

2 marks

Q4. Evaluate sin² 30° + cos² 30°.

Show answer

By sin² θ + cos² θ = 1, the answer is 1.

2 marks

Q5. Simplify (sec² θ − 1)/tan² θ.

Show answer

Since sec² θ − 1 = tan² θ, the value is 1 wherever defined.

3 marks

Q6. Prove (1 − sin² θ)/cos² θ = 1.

Show answer

Using 1 − sin² θ = cos² θ, the left side becomes cos² θ/cos² θ = 1, wherever defined.

3 marks

Q7. A 10 m ladder makes an angle of 60° with the ground. Find the height reached.

Show answer

Height = 10 sin 60° = 10√3/2 = 5√3 m ≈ 8.66 m.

3 marks

Q8. Convert 225° into radians.

Show answer

225 × π/180 = 5π/4 radians.

4 marks

Q9. A tower casts a 12 m shadow when the sun's angle of elevation is 30°. Find its height.

Show answer

tan 30° = h/12. Thus h = 12/√3 = 4√3 m ≈ 6.93 m.

4 marks

Q10. If sec θ = 13/12 and θ is acute, find tan θ.

Show answer

tan² θ = sec² θ − 1 = 169/144 − 1 = 25/144. Since θ is acute, tan θ = 5/12.

Challenge

Q11. Prove (sec θ − 1)(sec θ + 1) = tan² θ.

Show answer

Left side = sec² θ − 1 = tan² θ, using the fundamental identity.

13. Quick Revision Sheet

Six ratios: sin = O/H, cos = A/H, tan = O/A, cosec = H/O, sec = H/A, cot = A/O.

Core identities: sin² θ + cos² θ = 1; 1 + tan² θ = sec² θ; 1 + cot² θ = cosec² θ.

Reciprocal pairs: sin–cosec, cos–sec, tan–cot.

Quotients: tan θ = sin θ/cos θ; cot θ = cos θ/sin θ.

Complementary angles: sin(90° − θ) = cos θ; tan(90° − θ) = cot θ.

Degrees/radians: 180° = π radians.

Height and distance: tan θ = height/horizontal distance for the illustrated right triangle.
