Question Tags: Rules, Examples and Exercises

Question tags are short questions added to statements to check information, invite agreement or keep a conversation going. Learn how to form them correctly.

1. What Is a Question Tag?

A question tag is a short question placed at the end of a statement. It often checks whether something is true or invites the listener to agree.

  • You are ready, aren't you?
  • She can swim, can't she?
  • They didn't call, did they?

A comma usually separates the statement from its tag, and the tag ends with a question mark.

2. The Main Rule: Opposite Polarity

In the most common pattern, a positive statement takes a negative tag, while a negative statement takes a positive tag.

StatementQuestion tag
You are a student,aren't you?
He is not busy,is he?
They have finished,haven't they?
She doesn't drive,does she?
We can start now,can't we?
You won't forget,will you?

3. Use the Same Auxiliary Verb

If the statement already has a helping verb or modal verb, use that same verb in the tag.

  • Rita is singing, isn't she?
  • The children were playing, weren't they?
  • You have seen this film, haven't you?
  • He should apologise, shouldn't he?
  • We will meet tomorrow, won't we?

Keep the tense and modal meaning consistent between the statement and tag.

4. When There Is No Auxiliary Verb

In the simple present and simple past, use do, does or did when the statement has no auxiliary verb.

  • You like tea, don't you?
  • She works here, doesn't she?
  • They visited Jaipur, didn't they?
  • He didn't call, did he?

Remember to use the base form of the main verb in the statement's question structure when forming a full question, but the tag itself uses the appropriate auxiliary.

5. Choose the Correct Pronoun

The subject in the tag is usually a pronoun that matches the subject of the statement.

  • Meena is clever, isn't she?
  • Rahul and Aman are friends, aren't they?
  • The dog is hungry, isn't it?
  • Your parents have arrived, haven't they?

With this or that, the tag usually uses it: This is your bag, isn't it? With these or those, use they: Those are your books, aren't they?

6. Special Case: “I am”

The usual tag after a positive statement with I am is aren't I?

  • I am late, aren't I?
  • I am not disturbing you, am I?

Aren't I? is the standard tag form in this context, despite the apparent difference from am.

7. Negative Words in the Statement

Words such as never, nobody, no one, nothing, hardly, scarcely and rarely already make a statement negative in meaning. They normally take a positive tag.

  • He never complains, does he?
  • Nobody came, did they?
  • Nothing happened, did it?
  • She hardly speaks, does she?

Nobody and no one are commonly followed by they in the tag when referring to people.

8. Question Tags with “Let's” and Imperatives

Let's

Suggestions beginning with Let's usually take the tag shall we?

  • Let's begin the lesson, shall we?
  • Let's take a break, shall we?

Imperatives

Commands and requests often use will you? A polite request may also use could you? or would you?, depending on the sentence.

  • Close the door, will you?
  • Please help me, could you?
  • Don't be late, will you?

These forms depend on tone and context; not every imperative needs a tag.

9. Question Tags with “There” and “Have”

  • There is a problem, isn't there?
  • There weren't any seats, were there?
  • She has completed the task, hasn't she?
  • You have a bicycle, don't you?

When have is a main verb meaning possession, tag usage can vary by dialect. In common modern English, You have a bicycle, don't you? is natural. When have is an auxiliary in a perfect tense, use have/has in the tag.

10. Meaning and Intonation

Question tags can be used in different ways:

  • Checking information: You haven't met him before, have you? The speaker genuinely wants to know.
  • Inviting agreement: It's a lovely day, isn't it? The speaker expects agreement.

In speech, a rising tone often indicates a real question or uncertainty. A falling tone often suggests that the speaker expects agreement. Intonation can change the feel of the sentence even when the words stay the same.

11. Common Mistakes

  • Incorrect: She is a doctor, doesn't she? Correct: She is a doctor, isn't she?
  • Incorrect: He didn't come, didn't he? Correct: He didn't come, did he?
  • Incorrect: They play cricket, do they? (for the usual opposite-polarity tag) Correct: They play cricket, don't they?
  • Incorrect: Let's go, will we? Correct: Let's go, shall we?
  • Incorrect: Nobody called, didn't they? Usual form: Nobody called, did they?

14. Quick Revision

  • Positive statement → usually negative tag.
  • Negative statement → usually positive tag.
  • Use the same auxiliary or modal verb when one is present.
  • Use do/does/did when a simple present or past statement has no auxiliary.
  • Use a matching pronoun in the tag.
  • Remember special patterns: I am…, aren't I? and Let's…, shall we?
  • Words such as never and nobody normally take positive tags.

To form a question tag, first identify whether the statement is positive or negative. Then find the auxiliary verb and choose the correct pronoun.
