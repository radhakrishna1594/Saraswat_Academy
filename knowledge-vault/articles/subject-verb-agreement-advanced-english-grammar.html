Subject–Verb Agreement: Advanced Rules and Common Errors

A sentence sounds correct when its subject and verb agree. Revise the basic rule and learn the tricky cases involving joined subjects, indefinite pronouns, quantities, collective nouns and expressions that often confuse students.

1. What Is Subject–Verb Agreement?

Subject–verb agreement means that the verb must match its subject in number and person. A singular subject generally takes a singular verb, while a plural subject takes a plural verb.

  • The student works hard.
  • The students work hard.
  • She is ready.
  • They are ready.

In the simple present tense, remember that he, she and it usually take a verb ending in -s or -es: He reads, but they read.

2. Do Not Be Misled by Words Between the Subject and Verb

The verb agrees with the main subject, not with a noun that appears nearby in a phrase beginning with of, with, along with, as well as or together with.

  • The box of old books is heavy.
  • The captain, along with the players, is arriving.
  • The students, as well as their teacher, are attending.

To check the agreement, temporarily remove the extra phrase: “The box is heavy.” The main subject is box, not books.

3. Subjects Joined by “And”

Two or more subjects joined by and usually take a plural verb.

  • Ravi and Mohan are classmates.
  • Reading and writing improve communication.

However, when two words refer to one person, one item or one combined idea, a singular verb may be used.

  • Bread and butter is my usual breakfast.
  • The poet and teacher has arrived. (One person who is both poet and teacher.)

Compare: “The poet and the teacher have arrived.” The repeated article usually signals two different people.

4. Subjects Joined by “Either…or” and “Neither…nor”

When subjects are joined by either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also, or or, the verb generally agrees with the subject closest to it. This is often called the proximity rule.

  • Either the teacher or the students are presenting today.
  • Either the students or the teacher is presenting today.
  • Neither the manager nor the workers were available.
  • Not only the players but also the coach was congratulated.

When the result sounds awkward, rewrite the sentence to avoid placing a singular and plural subject together.

5. Indefinite Pronouns

Pronouns such as everyone, everybody, someone, somebody, anyone, anybody, no one, nobody, each and either are grammatically singular and usually take singular verbs.

  • Everyone is welcome.
  • Each of the answers has a reason.
  • Nobody knows the result.
  • Either of these routes leads to the station.

Both, few, many and several generally take plural verbs.

  • Both are correct.
  • Several have applied.

All, some, most and none depend on the noun or meaning they refer to: “Some of the water is missing,” but “Some of the bottles are missing.”

6. Collective Nouns

Words such as team, family, committee, class and audience name groups. In standard school grammar, they often take a singular verb when the group is considered as one unit.

  • The team is ready for the match.
  • The committee has made its decision.
  • Our family lives in Jaipur.

In some varieties of British English, a plural verb is possible when attention is on the members acting individually: “The team are changing their uniforms.” For school exercises, follow the convention specified by your textbook or examination board.

7. Amounts, Measurements and Time

When an amount, distance, period of time or sum of money is treated as one unit, it usually takes a singular verb.

  • Ten kilometres is a long distance to walk.
  • Five hundred rupees is enough for the ticket.
  • Two hours is not enough to complete the paper.

Use a plural verb when the individual items or units are being counted separately.

  • Two hours have passed since noon.
  • Ten kilometres were marked out in separate sections.

8. “A Number of” and “The Number of”

These two expressions take different verb forms.

  • A number of students are absent. (Meaning: several students.)
  • The number of absent students is increasing. (The number itself is one figure.)

Remember: a number of + plural noun + plural verb; the number of + plural noun + singular verb.

9. “There Is” and “There Are”

In sentences beginning with there or here, the verb agrees with the subject that follows it.

  • There is a book on the desk.
  • There are three books on the desk.
  • Here comes the bus.
  • Here come the buses.

In formal writing, avoid letting the first word of the sentence distract you from the real subject.

10. Titles, Names and Words Used as Subjects

A book title, film title, subject name or phrase used as one idea usually takes a singular verb, even if it looks plural.

  • Great Expectations is a well-known novel.
  • Mathematics is my favourite subject.
  • “Honesty” is an abstract noun.

Some nouns ending in -s, such as news, are normally singular: “The news is encouraging.” Other nouns, such as scissors and trousers, usually take plural verbs: “These scissors are sharp.”

11. Relative Clauses: “Who”, “Which” and “That”

The verb in a relative clause agrees with the noun or pronoun that who, which or that refers to.

  • She is one of the students who work hard. (The clause refers to students.)
  • She is the only student who works hard. (The clause refers to the only student.)
  • This is the book that explains the rule.

Pay attention to the intended meaning and the noun that the relative pronoun represents.

15. Quick Revision Checklist

  • Identify the main subject before choosing the verb.
  • Ignore extra phrases between the subject and verb.
  • Subjects joined by and usually take a plural verb.
  • With either…or and neither…nor, the verb generally agrees with the nearer subject.
  • Everyone, each, somebody and neither usually take singular verbs.
  • Amounts and periods of time take singular verbs when treated as one unit.
  • Remember the difference between a number of and the number of.
  • With there is/are, check the subject that follows the verb.

Before finalising a sentence, ask yourself: “Who or what is the subject?” Once you find it, choosing the correct verb becomes much easier.
