Simple, Compound and Complex Sentences

Sentences can be classified by how their clauses are arranged. Learn to recognise simple, compound and complex sentences, understand their structures, and combine ideas clearly.

1. What Is Sentence Structure?

A sentence expresses a complete thought. Its structure depends on the number and types of clauses it contains. A clause has a subject and a predicate. An independent clause can stand alone, while a dependent clause relies on another clause to complete its meaning.

There are three main structural types: simple, compound and complex sentences.

2. Simple Sentences

A simple sentence contains one independent clause. It has one complete subject–predicate structure, though the subject or predicate may be compound.

  • The baby slept.
  • Riya and Meena play chess. — compound subject.
  • The dog barked and chased the cat. — compound predicate.
  • After lunch, we returned to class. — the opening phrase does not make a second clause.

Remember: A simple sentence may be long. Its length does not determine its type; the number of independent and dependent clauses does.

3. Compound Sentences

A compound sentence contains at least two independent clauses joined appropriately. Each clause can stand as a sentence on its own.

Examples:

  • The rain stopped, and the children went outside.
  • I wanted to call you, but my phone battery was dead.
  • Hurry up, or you will miss the bus.
  • The shop was closed; we returned home.

Common coordinating conjunctions are for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so (often remembered as FANBOYS). A semicolon can also join closely related independent clauses.

4. Complex Sentences

A complex sentence contains one independent clause and at least one dependent clause.

  • We stayed inside because it was raining.
  • When the bell rang, the students left the classroom.
  • The book that you gave me is interesting.
  • If you practise regularly, you will improve.

Dependent clauses often begin with words such as because, although, when, while, if, unless, since, after, before, who, which and that. The word alone does not always determine the structure; examine how the clause works in the sentence.

5. Comparison at a Glance

TypeClause structureExample
SimpleOne independent clauseShe smiled.
CompoundTwo or more independent clausesShe smiled, and I waved.
ComplexOne independent clause plus at least one dependent clauseShe smiled when I waved.

6. How to Identify the Sentence Type

  1. Find the verbs and clauses. Look for each subject–predicate unit.
  2. Check whether each clause can stand alone. This helps you distinguish independent clauses from dependent clauses.
  3. Count and classify the clauses. One independent clause only means simple; two or more independent clauses means compound; an independent clause with one or more dependent clauses means complex.

Example: Although it was late, we continued working, and our teacher helped us. The sentence is compound-complex because it has two independent clauses and one dependent clause. This is an additional structure that combines features of compound and complex sentences.

7. Converting One Type into Another

The same idea can often be expressed using different sentence structures.

Simple to Compound

Simple: Being tired, Aman went to bed.

Compound: Aman was tired, so he went to bed.

Simple to Complex

Simple: In spite of the rain, the match continued.

Complex: Although it was raining, the match continued.

Compound to Complex

Compound: The sun set, and we returned home.

Complex: When the sun set, we returned home.

When rewriting a sentence, preserve its original meaning and choose a connector that expresses the correct relationship, such as time, reason, contrast or result.

8. Common Mistakes

  • Calling every long sentence complex: Length does not decide the type. Count and classify its clauses.
  • Confusing a phrase with a dependent clause: after the match is a phrase; after the match ended is a dependent clause.
  • Using a comma alone between independent clauses: Avoid a comma splice. Use a coordinating conjunction, a semicolon, or separate sentences.
  • Leaving a dependent clause alone: “Because the road was blocked.” is usually a fragment. Write, “We took another route because the road was blocked.”

9. Practice Exercise A: Identify the Type

Classify each sentence as simple, compound, complex or compound-complex.

  1. The birds sang beautifully.
  2. I called her, but she did not answer.
  3. We waited until the bus arrived.
  4. Although he was nervous, he gave the speech, and the audience applauded.
  5. My brother washed the dishes and cleaned the kitchen.
  6. If you finish early, you may read a book.
Show answers
  1. Simple — one independent clause.
  2. Compound — two independent clauses joined by but.
  3. Complex — one independent clause and the dependent clause “until the bus arrived”.
  4. Compound-complex — two independent clauses and one dependent clause.
  5. Simple — one independent clause with a compound predicate.
  6. Complex — one independent clause and the dependent clause “If you finish early”.

10. Practice Exercise B: Join the Ideas

Combine each pair into the type of sentence specified.

  1. Compound: It was getting dark. We went home.
  2. Complex: She practised every day. She wanted to improve.
  3. Complex: The bell rang. The students packed their bags.
Show sample answers
  1. It was getting dark, so we went home.
  2. She practised every day because she wanted to improve.
  3. When the bell rang, the students packed their bags.

Other answers are possible if they follow the required structure and preserve the meaning.

11. Quick Revision

  • Simple: one independent clause.
  • Compound: two or more independent clauses.
  • Complex: one independent clause and at least one dependent clause.
  • Compound-complex: at least two independent clauses and at least one dependent clause.
  • Identify clauses before deciding the sentence type.

Practise by underlining each clause in a sentence and marking it independent or dependent. This makes sentence classification much easier.
