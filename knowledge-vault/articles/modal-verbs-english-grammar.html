Modal Verbs: Can, Could, May, Might, Must and Should

Modal verbs help us express ability, permission, possibility, advice, obligation and willingness. Learn the meaning and use of common modals through clear examples and exercises.

1. What Are Modal Verbs?

Modal verbs are helping verbs used with a main verb to express ideas such as ability, possibility, permission, advice or necessity. Common modal verbs include can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will and would.

  • I can swim. — ability
  • You should rest. — advice
  • It might rain. — possibility
  • You must wear a helmet. — strong obligation

2. Basic Rules of Modal Verbs

  • Use a modal verb with the base form of the main verb: She can sing.
  • Do not add -s or -es to the main verb after a modal: He can play, not He can plays.
  • Usually, do not put to between a modal and the main verb: We must leave, not We must to leave.
  • Make a negative by adding not after the modal: cannot, should not, might not.
  • Form most questions by placing the modal before the subject: Can you help me?

There are related expressions such as ought to, have to and be able to that work somewhat differently from central modal verbs.

3. Can and Could

Can

Can is used for present ability, informal permission and general possibility.

  • Ability: My sister can drive.
  • Permission: Can I use your pen?
  • Possibility: Roads can be slippery after rain.

Could

Could can express past general ability, a polite request or a possible situation.

  • Past ability: I could read when I was five.
  • Polite request: Could you please open the window?
  • Possibility: We could visit the museum tomorrow.

For a specific successful action in the past, English often uses was/were able to rather than could: Despite the traffic, we were able to reach the station on time.

4. May and Might

May is commonly used for formal permission and possibility. Might often suggests a more tentative possibility, though the difference depends on context.

  • Permission: May I come in?
  • Possibility: It may rain this evening.
  • Less certain possibility: She might join us later.
  • Possible past situation: He might have missed the bus.

Do not assume that might always means something is impossible or that it always expresses a fixed lower level of probability than may. Context matters.

5. Must and Have To

Must expresses strong obligation, necessity or a logical conclusion. Have to is also used for obligation and is especially common when the requirement comes from a rule or situation.

  • Obligation: You must follow the safety instructions.
  • External requirement: We have to wear our school uniform.
  • Logical conclusion: The lights are on; someone must be at home.

Important difference: Must not usually means that something is prohibited. Do not have to means that something is not necessary.

  • You must not park here. — parking is prohibited.
  • You do not have to come early. — coming early is optional.

6. Should and Ought To

Should and ought to commonly express advice, recommendations or what is expected to happen.

  • Advice: You should drink enough water.
  • Recommendation: We should check our answers.
  • Expectation: The parcel should arrive tomorrow.
  • Ought to: You ought to apologise for the mistake.

For ordinary school grammar, remember that should is followed by the base verb, while ought is followed by to + base verb.

7. Will and Would

Will

Will is often used for predictions, decisions, promises and willingness.

  • Prediction: It will be sunny tomorrow.
  • Promise: I will help you with the project.
  • Willingness: I will carry that bag for you.

Would

Would is used in polite requests, hypothetical situations, and some reported statements. It can also describe repeated past actions in a suitable context.

  • Polite request: Would you mind closing the door?
  • Hypothetical situation: I would travel more if I had extra time.
  • Reported speech: She said that she would return soon.
  • Past habit: During the holidays, we would play outside every evening.

8. Shall and Other Useful Expressions

Shall is used in offers and suggestions, especially with I and we in questions.

  • Offer: Shall I carry your books?
  • Suggestion: Shall we begin the lesson?

Other expressions can communicate modal meanings too:

  • Be able to — ability: She is able to solve the puzzle.
  • Have to — necessity: I have to submit the form today.
  • Need to — necessity: You need to revise these rules.
  • Ought to — advice or duty: We ought to respect others.

9. Modal Verbs for Past Meaning

To talk about past possibilities, regrets, deductions or actions that did not happen, use a modal followed by have + past participle (V3).

FormCommon meaningExample
could have + V3Past possibility or an unused opportunityYou could have called me.
might have + V3Possibility about the pastShe might have forgotten.
must have + V3Strong deduction about the pastThey must have left already.
should have + V3Advice or duty that was not followedI should have studied earlier.
would have + V3Imagined or conditional past resultI would have helped if I had known.

These forms do not all mean the same thing. Read the whole sentence to understand whether it expresses possibility, deduction, regret or a conditional result.

10. Questions and Negative Forms

PositiveNegativeQuestion
She can swim.She cannot swim.Can she swim?
He should go.He should not go.Should he go?
They might come.They might not come.Might they come?
You must wait.You must not enter.Must we wait?

Some negative forms have special meanings. For example, cannot means not able or not possible, while must not usually expresses prohibition.

11. Common Mistakes

  • Incorrect: He can speaks English. Correct: He can speak English.
  • Incorrect: You should to study. Correct: You should study.
  • Incorrect: She doesn't can drive. Correct: She cannot drive.
  • Incorrect: Must I to go? Correct: Must I go?
  • Incorrect meaning: You must not attend means attendance is optional. Correct distinction: You must not attend = you are not allowed to attend; you do not have to attend = attendance is not required.

12. Practice Exercise A: Fill in the Blanks

Choose a suitable modal verb for each sentence. More than one answer may be possible in some contexts.

  1. You ___ wear a helmet while riding a motorcycle. (strong advice or requirement)
  2. ___ I borrow your dictionary? (polite permission)
  3. When she was six, she ___ read simple stories. (past ability)
  4. The sky is dark. It ___ rain soon. (possibility)
  5. You ___ not use your phone during the examination. (prohibition)
  6. ___ we start the meeting now? (suggestion)
  7. You look unwell. You ___ see a doctor. (advice)
  8. He is not answering. He ___ have left his phone at home. (past possibility)
Show sample answers
  1. must / should
  2. May / Could
  3. could
  4. may / might
  5. must
  6. Shall
  7. should
  8. might / may

13. Practice Exercise B: Correct the Sentences

  1. She can sings beautifully.
  2. You must to finish your work.
  3. He doesn't can attend the class.
  4. You must not bring your notebook means it is not necessary to bring it.
Show answers
  1. She can sing beautifully.
  2. You must finish your work.
  3. He cannot attend the class.
  4. Use “You do not have to bring your notebook” if bringing it is not necessary. “You must not bring your notebook” means bringing it is prohibited.

14. Quick Revision

  • Can/could: ability, permission, requests or possibility.
  • May/might: permission or possibility.
  • Must/have to: necessity or obligation; must can also express a strong deduction.
  • Should/ought to: advice, recommendation or expectation.
  • Will/would: predictions, willingness, polite requests and hypothetical situations.
  • Use the base form after most modal verbs: can go, should study, might rain.
  • Use modal + have + V3 for many modal meanings about the past.

Choose a modal based on meaning, not simply because it appears in a list. The context tells you whether you need to express ability, permission, possibility, advice or obligation.
