Structure of the Atmosphere and All Its Layers

Earth is surrounded by a blanket of gases called the atmosphere. It gives us air to breathe, helps regulate temperature and protects life from many harmful effects coming from space.

The atmosphere does not have the same conditions at every height. As we move upward, air becomes thinner and temperature changes. Scientists divide it into five main layers.

What Is the Atmosphere?

The atmosphere is the mixture of gases held around Earth by gravity. Dry air near Earth's surface contains about 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen, with small amounts of argon, carbon dioxide and other gases. Water vapour varies from place to place.

Most of the atmosphere's mass lies close to Earth's surface. There is no sharp outer edge: the air gradually becomes thinner and merges with space.

The Five Main Layers at a Glance

Learn the layers from the ground upward. The heights shown are approximate because the boundaries change with latitude, season and solar activity.

Easy order to remember: Troposphere → Stratosphere → Mesosphere → Thermosphere → Exosphere.

1. Troposphere — The Weather Layer

Surface to about 8–18 km (often taken as ~12 km)

The troposphere is the lowest layer and the one in which we live. It contains most of the atmosphere's mass and nearly all of its water vapour.

  • Clouds, rain, snow, winds and most other weather events occur here.
  • Air pressure and air density decrease as altitude increases.
  • Temperature usually decreases with height, although local conditions can vary.

The troposphere is generally thinner near the poles and taller near the equator. That is why its upper boundary is given as a range rather than one fixed height.

2. Stratosphere — The Ozone Layer

About 12–50 km

The stratosphere lies above the troposphere. It contains the ozone layer, where ozone gas absorbs much of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation.

  • The ozone layer protects living things from much of the Sun's harmful UV radiation.
  • Temperature generally increases with height in this layer because ozone absorbs ultraviolet energy.
  • Some aircraft fly in the lower stratosphere to avoid much of the weather below.

3. Mesosphere — The Coldest Layer

About 50–85 km

The mesosphere is above the stratosphere. It is usually the coldest atmospheric layer, especially near its upper boundary.

  • Many meteoroids heat up and break apart here, producing the streaks of light we call shooting stars.
  • Temperature generally falls as height increases through the mesosphere.
  • The air is extremely thin, so this region is difficult for ordinary aircraft to reach and for satellites to orbit in for long periods.

4. Thermosphere — Very High Temperatures

About 85–600 km (boundary varies)

In the thermosphere, gases absorb high-energy radiation from the Sun. The measured temperature can become very high, but the air is so thin that it would not transfer heat to a person in the same way that hot air near the ground does.

  • Many auroras occur in this region when charged particles interact with gases in the upper atmosphere.
  • The ionosphere, an electrically charged region important for radio communication, overlaps parts of the mesosphere and thermosphere and can extend higher.
  • The International Space Station orbits Earth within the thermosphere.

5. Exosphere — The Outermost Layer

Roughly 600 km upward, gradually merging into space

The exosphere is the outermost part of Earth's atmosphere. Its gas particles are extremely far apart, and some can travel long distances without colliding with other particles.

  • Hydrogen and helium are among the gases found there.
  • There is no sharp line where the atmosphere suddenly ends; it gradually becomes thinner and blends into space.
  • Some high-altitude satellites orbit in or near this region, although satellites occupy a wide range of altitudes.

How Does Temperature Change in the Atmosphere?

Temperature does not simply keep falling as we move away from Earth. It changes direction from one layer to another.

Why Is the Atmosphere Important?

  • It supplies essential gases: oxygen for many living things and carbon dioxide for photosynthesis.
  • It supports weather and the water cycle: clouds and precipitation form mainly in the troposphere.
  • It helps regulate temperature: greenhouse gases help keep Earth warm enough for life.
  • It filters harmful radiation: ozone in the stratosphere absorbs much of the Sun's harmful UV radiation.
  • It provides protection: atmospheric gases cause many small meteoroids to heat up and break apart before reaching the ground.

Common Misunderstandings

  • “The ozone layer is a separate atmospheric layer.” It is a region with higher ozone concentration within the stratosphere, not a sixth main layer.
  • “All meteors burn up in the mesosphere.” Many do, but some begin to ablate at different heights and larger objects can survive to the ground.
  • “The atmosphere ends exactly at 600 km.” It has no sharp edge; the exosphere gradually merges with space.
  • “The thermosphere feels extremely hot.” Its particles can have very high temperatures, but the air is extremely thin.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many main layers does Earth's atmosphere have?

It is commonly divided into five main layers: troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere and exosphere.

Which layer do we live in?

We live at Earth's surface, within the troposphere, the lowest atmospheric layer.

Which layer contains the ozone layer?

The ozone layer is mainly in the stratosphere.

Which is the coldest atmospheric layer?

The mesosphere is generally the coldest layer, with temperatures near its upper boundary often dropping to around −90°C or lower depending on conditions.

Where do most weather events occur?

Most weather events occur in the troposphere, where most water vapour and clouds are found.
