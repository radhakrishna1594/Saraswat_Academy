Adjectives in English Grammar: Types and Examples

Compare these two sentences: “I saw a dog” and “I saw a small, playful dog.” The second sentence gives us a clearer picture. Words such as small and playful describe the noun dog. These describing words are called adjectives.

1. What is an adjective?

An adjective is a word that describes a noun or pronoun, or gives us more information about it.

SentenceAdjectiveWhat it tells us
We live in a quiet street.quietWhat kind of street
She has three pencils.threeHow many pencils
I need some water.someHow much water / an amount
That house is old.thatWhich house (often classified as a determiner in modern grammar)

Adjectives often come before a noun—“a bright light”—but they can also follow a linking verb: “The light is bright.”

2. Descriptive adjectives

Descriptive adjectives tell us what someone or something is like. They can describe colour, size, shape, age, condition, personality or quality.

Kind of descriptionExamplesExample sentence
Colourblue, green, goldenHe wore a blue shirt.
Sizesmall, huge, tallA huge tree stood nearby.
Shaperound, square, narrowShe drew a round circle.
Ageyoung, old, modernThey live in an old house.
Quality or feelingkind, honest, cheerfulOur teacher is kind.

3. Quantity, number and demonstrative words

Traditional school grammar often groups these words with adjectives. In modern grammar, many of them are called determiners when they come before a noun. Learn their use and notice the difference.

GroupExamplesSentence
Quantitysome, much, little, enoughWe have enough time.
Numberone, two, several, manySeveral students volunteered.
Demonstrativethis, that, these, thoseThose shoes are new.
Possessivemy, your, his, her, our, theirMy notebook is missing.
Interrogativewhich, what, whoseWhich colour do you prefer?

Words such as three and many can give information about number, while my shows who something belongs to.

4. Adjectives in different positions

An adjective can appear in more than one position in a sentence.

  • Before the noun: We visited a beautiful garden.
  • After a linking verb: The garden looks beautiful.
  • After the noun in certain expressions: The people present voted. (Here, present means those who were there.)

Common linking verbs include be, seem, become, look, feel, smell, sound and taste. For example, “The tea tastes sweet.”

5. Degrees of comparison

Adjectives can compare people or things. The three commonly taught degrees are positive, comparative and superlative.

PositiveComparativeSuperlativeExample
talltallertallestRavi is tall. Amit is taller. Dev is the tallest.
smallsmallersmallestThis box is smaller than that one.
happyhappierhappiestShe looks happier today.
beautifulmore beautifulmost beautifulIt is the most beautiful painting here.
goodbetterbestThis is better than the old plan.
badworseworstThat was the worst delay of the week.

Rules to remember:

  • Many short adjectives take -er and -est: fast, faster, fastest.
  • For adjectives ending in consonant + y, change y to i: easy, easier, easiest.
  • Some short adjectives double the final consonant: big, bigger, biggest.
  • Longer adjectives often use more and most: careful, more careful, most careful.
  • Some forms are irregular: good, better, best; bad, worse, worst.

6. Avoid common comparison mistakes

IncorrectCorrectWhy?
more tallertallerDo not normally use more with an -er comparative.
most fastestfastestDo not normally use most with an -est superlative.
She is senior than me.She is senior to me.Senior is commonly followed by to, not than.
Of the two, this is the best.Of the two, this is the better.In traditional school grammar, use the comparative for two choices.

For comparisons, use than: “My bag is lighter than yours.” For a group of three or more, the superlative is often appropriate: “This is the lightest bag of all.”

7. Order of adjectives

When several adjectives come before a noun, English usually follows a natural order. We do not often say them in a random order.

Natural: a lovely small old wooden box

A common guide is: opinion → size → age → shape → colour → origin → material → purpose → noun.

Another example: a beautiful large Indian silk scarf.

You do not need to use many adjectives in every sentence. One or two well-chosen words often sound clearer.

8. Adjectives and adverbs are different

An adjective usually describes a noun or pronoun. An adverb often describes a verb, adjective or another adverb.

AdjectiveAdverbExample
carefulcarefullyShe is a careful driver. She drives carefully.
quickquicklyHe gave a quick answer. He answered quickly.
goodwellThat is a good result. You did well.

Not every adverb ends in -ly, and some -ly words are adjectives, such as friendly. Learn the word's job in the sentence.

9. Practice questions

  1. Identify the adjective: “The clever student solved the puzzle.”
  2. Fill in the blank: “This is a ______ flower.” Use an adjective of colour.
  3. Write the comparative and superlative forms of happy.
  4. Choose the correct form: “My bag is (heavy / heavier) than yours.”
  5. Correct the mistake: “This is the most easiest question.”
  6. Choose the adjective: “She sings (beautiful / beautifully).”
  7. Complete: “Good, ______, best.”
  8. Find the adjective after a linking verb: “The soup smells delicious.”
Show answers and explanations
  1. clever — it describes the student.
  2. Answers may vary: red, blue, yellow, white, etc.
  3. happier, happiest.
  4. heavier — the sentence compares two bags.
  5. “This is the easiest question.”
  6. beautifully — it describes how she sings.
  7. better.
  8. delicious — it describes the soup after the linking verb smells.
