Imperative Sentences: Commands, Requests and Advice

Imperative sentences are used to tell someone what to do or not to do. They can express commands, instructions, requests, advice, warnings and invitations. Learn how to form them and make them sound polite.

1. What is an imperative sentence?

An imperative sentence gives a command, instruction, request, warning, piece of advice or invitation. The subject is usually the person being spoken to, you, but it is normally left unstated.

  • Open your book. (instruction)
  • Please wait here. (request)
  • Take care of your health. (advice)
  • Watch out! (warning)

Imperative sentences usually begin with the base form of a verb and often end with a full stop. A strong command or warning may end with an exclamation mark.

2. Structure of imperative sentences

The common pattern is base verb + the rest of the sentence. The subject you is understood.

PatternExample
V1 + objectClose the door.
Please + V1Please bring your notebook.
V1 + adverb or phraseSpeak clearly.
Be + adjectiveBe careful.
Let's + V1Let's begin the lesson.

Use the base form of the verb: say Go straight, not Goes straight or To go straight.

3. Commands and instructions

A command tells someone to do something. An instruction explains what action to take, often step by step.

  • Stand in a straight line.
  • Write your name at the top of the page.
  • Turn off the lights before leaving.
  • Mix the flour with water.

Commands can sound direct. In formal or everyday situations, a polite word or phrase can soften them.

4. Requests and polite imperatives

A request asks someone to do something. Adding please, kindly or a polite opening can make the sentence more courteous.

Direct formMore polite form
Give me the file.Please give me the file.
Wait here.Could you please wait here?
Explain this question.Would you please explain this question?

Although “Could you please...?” and “Would you please...?” are grammatically questions, they commonly function as polite requests.

5. Advice, suggestions and invitations

Imperatives can also guide, encourage or invite someone.

  • Eat a balanced diet. (advice)
  • Keep practising every day. (encouragement)
  • Let's take a short break. (suggestion including the speaker)
  • Come in and make yourself comfortable. (invitation)
  • Have a seat. (invitation)

Let's means “let us” and is used to suggest that the speaker and listener do something together. The negative form is Let's not argue.

6. Negative imperative sentences

To tell someone not to do something, use do not (don't) + base verb.

Positive imperativeNegative imperative
Touch the switch.Do not touch the switch.
Run in the corridor.Don't run in the corridor.
Forget your homework.Don't forget your homework.
Be careless.Don't be careless.

For be, say Don't be late. In notices and formal instructions, Do not enter is common.

7. Imperatives with emphasis and softening words

Words such as just, please, always and never can change the tone or emphasis of an imperative.

  • Just follow the instructions.
  • Always check your answers.
  • Never share your password.
  • Please do sit down. (emphatic invitation)

Use strong forms such as Never touch that wire! when a warning needs emphasis. Choose tone and punctuation to suit the situation.

8. Imperative sentences and other sentence types

TypeExamplePurpose
DeclarativeYou should close the door.States advice or information.
InterrogativeCould you close the door?Asks a question; often a polite request.
ImperativeClose the door, please.Gives a request or instruction.
ExclamatoryWhat a noisy door!Expresses strong feeling.

The speaker's intention matters. A sentence with a question mark can still function as a request, even though its grammatical form is interrogative.

9. Common mistakes

IncorrectCorrectReason
To open your book.Open your book.Begin a direct imperative with the base verb.
Opens the door.Open the door.Do not add -s to the imperative verb.
Don't to run.Don't run.Use the base verb after don't.
Not be late.Don't be late.Use don't before a negative imperative.
Let's to start.Let's start.Use the base verb after let's.
Please you sit down.Please sit down.The subject you is normally understood and omitted.

10. Practice exercises

Identify the purpose or correct the imperative. Try first, then open the answer.

  1. Identify the purpose: Please pass me the salt.
    Show answer

    Request — it politely asks someone to do something.

  2. Change to a negative imperative: Touch the wet paint.
    Show answer

    Do not touch the wet paint.

  3. Correct: To turn left at the crossing.
    Show answer

    Turn left at the crossing.

  4. Complete the suggestion: ___ go for a walk.
    Show answer

    Let's go for a walk.

  5. Make this more polite: Give me your notebook.
    Show answer

    Please give me your notebook. Another option: “Could you please give me your notebook?”

  6. Correct: Don't to shout in class.
    Show answer

    Don't shout in class.

  7. Identify the purpose: Wear a helmet while riding.
    Show answer

    Advice or instruction — it tells someone what they should do for safety.

  8. Make a negative imperative: Be careless.
    Show answer

    Don't be careless.

  9. Correct: Let's to begin the test.
    Show answer

    Let's begin the test.

  10. Write an imperative warning about a hot surface.
    Show answer

    Example: Don't touch the hot surface!

11. Quick revision

  • Imperatives give commands, instructions, requests, advice, warnings or invitations.
  • The subject “you” is usually understood and omitted.
  • Use the base verb: Open the book.
  • Use please or a polite question form to soften a request.
  • Use don't + V1 for negative imperatives: Don't run.
  • Use let's + V1 to make a suggestion that includes the speaker.
