Phrases and Clauses: Differences, Types and Examples

A phrase and a clause are both groups of words, but they do not work in the same way. Learn how to identify them, understand their types, and use them correctly in sentences.

1. What Is a Phrase?

A phrase is a group of words that works as a unit in a sentence but does not contain its own complete subject–predicate structure. A phrase does not express a complete thought by itself.

Examples: in the garden, a very tall boy, after the match, and has been working.

Notice that a phrase may contain a verb, as in has been working. The important point is that the phrase does not form a complete subject–predicate clause on its own.

2. What Is a Clause?

A clause is a group of words that contains a subject and a predicate, usually including a verb. A clause may express a complete thought or may depend on another clause to complete its meaning.

  • The children laughed. — subject: the children; predicate: laughed.
  • because the children laughed — subject: the children; verb: laughed, but the group depends on another clause.

4. Types of Phrases

Noun Phrase

A noun phrase acts as a noun and may include a noun with words describing or limiting it.

Example: The little girl in the blue dress won the prize.

Verb Phrase

A verb phrase contains a main verb and may include one or more helping verbs.

Example: They have been practising for the match.

Adjective Phrase

An adjective phrase describes a noun or pronoun.

Example: The road full of potholes needs repair.

Adverb Phrase

An adverb phrase tells us more about a verb, adjective, or adverb, often answering how, when, or where.

Example: She completed the work with great care.

Prepositional Phrase

A prepositional phrase begins with a preposition and includes its object.

Example: The cat slept under the table.

5. Types of Clauses

Independent or Main Clause

An independent clause expresses a complete thought and can stand alone as a sentence.

Example: The sun has set.

Dependent or Subordinate Clause

A dependent clause has a subject and a verb but cannot normally stand alone as a complete sentence. It depends on a main clause.

Example: although the sun was shining. Complete sentence: We stayed indoors although the sun was shining.

Noun Clause

A noun clause performs the work of a noun.

Example: I know that she is honest.

Adjective Clause

An adjective clause describes a noun or pronoun. It often begins with who, whom, whose, which or that.

Example: The player who scored the goal is my friend.

Adverb Clause

An adverb clause adds information about time, reason, condition, contrast, or another circumstance.

Example: We will start when the teacher arrives.

7. Common Mistakes

  • Mistake: Thinking every group of words with a verb is a clause. Remember: A clause has a subject–predicate structure; a verb phrase alone is not a clause.
  • Mistake: Treating a dependent clause as a complete sentence. Incorrect: Because it was raining. Correct: We cancelled the match because it was raining.
  • Mistake: Assuming every phrase contains no verb. Verb phrases can contain verbs; they simply do not make a complete clause by themselves.

Other grammatically correct answers are possible.

10. Quick Revision

  • A phrase works as a unit but does not have its own complete subject–predicate structure.
  • A clause has a subject and a predicate.
  • An independent clause can stand alone as a sentence.
  • A dependent clause needs a main clause to complete its meaning.
  • Noun, adjective, and adverb clauses perform different jobs in a sentence.

To identify a clause, look for its subject and predicate first. Then ask whether it expresses a complete thought on its own.
