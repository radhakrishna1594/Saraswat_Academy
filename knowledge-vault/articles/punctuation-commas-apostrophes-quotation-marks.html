Punctuation: Commas, Apostrophes and Quotation Marks

Punctuation helps readers understand where ideas pause, how words belong together and when someone is speaking. Learn three important punctuation marks and practise using them accurately.

1. What Is Punctuation?

Punctuation is the use of marks in writing to make meaning clear and organise sentences. A full stop ends a statement, a question mark ends a direct question, and a comma can separate items or mark a pause in sentence structure.

Compare these examples:

  • Let's eat, Grandma!
  • Let's eat Grandma!

The comma in the first sentence shows that Grandma is being addressed. Without it, the sentence has a very different and unintended meaning.

2. Using Commas in a List

Use commas to separate three or more items in a series.

  • We bought pencils, notebooks and erasers.
  • My favourite subjects are English, mathematics, science and history.
  • She packed shirts, shoes, a towel and a water bottle.

A comma before and in the final item is called the Oxford comma. It is used in many styles to prevent confusion, though some style guides omit it when the meaning is already clear. Be consistent with the style required by your school or publication.

3. Commas After Introductory Words and Phrases

A comma often follows an introductory word, phrase or dependent clause, especially when it helps the reader see where the main statement begins.

  • However, we decided to continue.
  • After the rain stopped, the children went outside.
  • In the morning, I revise my lessons.
  • To avoid confusion, read the instructions carefully.

Short introductory phrases do not always require a comma, but using one can improve clarity.

4. Commas with Names, Direct Address and Extra Information

Use a comma when directly addressing someone.

  • Riya, please open the window.
  • Could you help me, Arjun?
  • Thank you, everyone, for your support.

Use commas around extra information that could be removed without destroying the basic meaning of the sentence.

  • Jaipur, the capital of Rajasthan, attracts many visitors.
  • My brother, who lives in Delhi, is visiting us.

Do not place commas around information that is essential to identify the person or thing: “Students who practise regularly improve their skills.” The clause identifies which students are meant.

5. Avoid the Comma Splice

A comma splice occurs when two complete sentences are joined using only a comma. Correct it by using a full stop, a semicolon, or a comma followed by a suitable conjunction.

IncorrectCorrect alternatives
The bell rang, the students left.The bell rang. The students left.
I was tired, I finished the work.I was tired, but I finished the work.
It was late, we went home.It was late; we went home.

6. Apostrophes for Contractions

An apostrophe shows that letters have been omitted in a contraction.

do notdon't
cannotcan't

would notwouldn't

Contractions are common in conversation and informal writing. In formal academic work, use full forms when the context or required style calls for them.

7. Apostrophes to Show Possession

An apostrophe can show that something belongs to someone or something.

  • The girl's bag (one girl)
  • The girls' bags (more than one girl)
  • The children's books (irregular plural)
  • James's notebook (a common modern style for a singular name ending in s)
  • The teachers' room (a room used by several teachers)

For most singular nouns, add 's. For regular plural nouns ending in s, add an apostrophe after the s. Irregular plural nouns that do not end in s usually take 's, as in children's.

8. Its or It's? Their or They're?

These pairs are often confused because the apostrophe does not always indicate possession.

  • It's means it is or it has: It's raining. / It's been a long day.
  • Its shows possession: The dog wagged its tail.
  • They're means they are: They're ready.
  • Their shows possession: Their classroom is upstairs.
  • There refers to a place or introduces a statement: Put it there. / There are two books.
  • You're means you are; your shows possession.

Quick test: replace it's with it is. If the sentence still makes sense, the contraction may be correct. If not, you probably need its.

9. Quotation Marks for Direct Speech

Quotation marks show the exact words spoken or written by someone. In many school writing styles, double quotation marks are used for direct speech.

  • Neha said, “I am ready.”
  • “Where are you going?” asked the teacher.
  • “Please sit down,” said the librarian.

Use a comma to introduce a quotation when the sentence structure requires it. Capitalise the first word of a complete quoted sentence.

10. Punctuation with Quotation Marks

Placement conventions differ between British and American styles. Follow the style your school or publisher expects, and keep it consistent.

  • Direct question inside the quotation: She asked, “Are you ready?”
  • Statement followed by a reporting clause: “I am ready,” she said.
  • Quotation followed by a question about the quoted words: Did he really say “I refuse”?

In common American style, commas and full stops usually go inside closing quotation marks. In common British style, punctuation placement may depend on whether the punctuation belongs to the quoted material. The examples above illustrate the meaning-based approach where appropriate.

11. Quotation Marks for Titles and Special Words

Quotation marks may be used for short works such as poems, short stories, articles and individual chapters, depending on the style guide.

  • We read the poem “Daffodils” in class.
  • Have you read the article “How Plants Make Food”?
  • The word “generous” describes someone who is willing to give.

Longer works such as books and films are often italicised or underlined in handwritten work. Follow the formatting convention your teacher specifies.

12. Common Punctuation Mistakes

IncorrectCorrectRule
My friend, is helpful.My friend is helpful.Do not separate a simple subject from its verb with a comma.
Its a sunny day.It's a sunny day.Use the contraction for “it is”.
The dogs bone is missing.The dog's bone is missing.Use an apostrophe for singular possession.
Ravi said “I am ready”.Ravi said, “I am ready.”Use suitable punctuation to introduce direct speech.
We need paper, pens, and, glue.We need paper, pens and glue.Do not place commas randomly around a conjunction.
Its tail is long, its a friendly dog.Its tail is long; it's a friendly dog.Distinguish possession from contraction and avoid a comma splice.

13. Practice Exercise A: Add Commas

Rewrite each sentence with commas where needed.

  1. Before we left we checked the doors.
  2. My cousin who lives in Ajmer is visiting us.
  3. Rohan please bring your notebook.
  4. We bought apples bananas oranges and grapes.
  5. However the team did not give up.
Show answers
  1. Before we left, we checked the doors.
  2. My cousin, who lives in Ajmer, is visiting us. (This punctuation assumes the information is extra, not essential.)
  3. Rohan, please bring your notebook.
  4. We bought apples, bananas, oranges and grapes.
  5. However, the team did not give up.

14. Practice Exercise B: Choose the Correct Word

  1. (Its / It's) a good day for a walk.
  2. The bird spread (it's / its) wings.
  3. (Their / They're / There) waiting outside.
  4. The (students / student's / students') classroom is on the first floor. (Several students share it.)
  5. (Your / You're) welcome to join us.
Show answers

  2. its
  3. They're
  4. students' classroom
  5. You're

15. Practice Exercise C: Correct the Punctuation

  1. Meera said “I have completed my homework”.
  2. The cat licked it's paw.
  3. We were late, we missed the bus.
  4. My brothers bicycle is new.
  5. Lets begin the activity.
Show answers
  1. Meera said, “I have completed my homework.”
  2. The cat licked its paw.
  3. We were late, so we missed the bus. (A full stop or semicolon is also possible.)
  4. My brother's bicycle is new.
  5. Let's begin the activity.

16. Quick Revision

  • Use commas to separate list items, introduce some opening phrases and mark direct address.
  • Do not join two complete sentences with only a comma.
  • Use apostrophes for contractions and possession, but do not add them to ordinary plurals.
  • It's means “it is” or “it has”; its shows possession.
  • Quotation marks identify exact speech and may mark titles of short works.
  • Follow one punctuation style consistently, especially for quotation marks.

Good punctuation is not decoration; it helps your reader understand exactly what you mean. Read your writing aloud and check whether each mark makes the sentence clearer.
