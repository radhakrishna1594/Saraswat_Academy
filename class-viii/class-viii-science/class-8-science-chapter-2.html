Chapter Guide

Chapter Overview

Students learn about different types of microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi and algae, and are introduced to viruses as microscopic, acellular entities. The chapter shows that microorganisms are found almost everywhere and can be both useful and harmful.

The chapter explores the useful roles of microorganisms in everyday life. For example, yeast helps dough become soft and fluffy, while bacteria such as Lactobacillus are involved in fermentation used to make curd, idli and dosa. Microorganisms also help decompose plant and animal waste and convert it into nutrient-rich manure.

Overall, the chapter helps students understand how these invisible organisms interact with humans, plants, animals and the environment. It develops an appreciation for the important role microorganisms play in food production, decomposition, nutrient recycling and our daily lives.

Learn Clearly

Important Concepts and notes

1. Microorganisms

Microorganisms are very small living organisms that cannot be seen with the naked eye. They are also called microbes. They are found in water, soil, air and even inside the bodies of plants and animals.

A microscope is used to observe microorganisms. The textbook explains that the microscope opened up a hidden world of tiny living organisms.

2. Types of Microorganisms

The chapter introduces several groups of microorganisms:

  • Protozoa: Usually single-celled organisms such as Amoeba and Paramecium.
  • Algae: Some are microscopic and contain chlorophyll.
  • Fungi: May be unicellular or multicellular. Yeast and mould are examples.
  • Bacteria: Usually single-celled microorganisms with different shapes.

Observations of pond water and soil suspension in the chapter demonstrate the presence of different microorganisms.

3. Unicellular and Multicellular Organisms

  • Unicellular organisms are made up of a single cell.
  • Multicellular organisms are made up of many cells.
  • Bacteria and protozoa are examples of unicellular microorganisms.
  • Yeast is a unicellular fungus, while mould is a multicellular fungus.

A single cell performs all necessary life functions in a unicellular organism, whereas cells in multicellular organisms may perform specialised functions.

4. Cell as the Basic Unit of Life

The cell is considered the basic unit of life because the bodies of all living organisms are made up of one or more cells.

A typical cell has a cell membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus. Plant, fungal and bacterial cells have a cell wall. However, bacteria do not have a well-defined nucleus; instead, they have a nucleoid.

5. Microorganisms in Everyday Life

Microorganisms are not always harmful. Many of them are beneficial and are important in food production, agriculture and environmental processes.

6. Role of Yeast in Fermentation

Yeast is a type of fungus. It grows well in warm conditions and breaks down food during respiration. This process releases carbon dioxide, which forms bubbles and makes dough soft and fluffy.

Yeast is therefore used in making bread, cakes and other baked foods. Some bacteria such as Lactobacillus help in the fermentation of foods such as curd, idli, dosa and bhatura.

7. Lactobacillus and Curd Formation

Lactobacillus is a bacterium involved in curd formation. The chapter demonstrates the importance of temperature by comparing milk kept in warm and cool conditions.

8. Decomposition

Decomposition is the process in which microorganisms break down complex substances in plant and animal waste into simpler substances.

Fungi and bacteria help decompose fallen leaves, plant waste and animal waste. The resulting nutrient-rich material can form manure, which improves soil fertility. Microorganisms therefore help recycle nutrients and clean the environment.

9. Microorganisms and Soil Fertility

Some microorganisms live in the root nodules of leguminous plants such as peas, beans and lentils. They trap nitrogen from the air and help increase soil fertility.

10. Spirulina

Spirulina is a microalga described in the chapter as a nutritious food source. It is rich in protein and is also described as a source of vitamin B12. The chapter highlights its potential for food security and livelihood development.

11. Viruses

Viruses are microscopic and acellular. Unlike the microorganisms discussed as cellular life forms, viruses multiply when they enter a living cell. They can infect plants, animals or bacterial cells and may cause diseases.

12. Helpful and Harmful Microorganisms

Microorganisms can be both beneficial and harmful. Helpful microorganisms participate in fermentation, decomposition, nutrient recycling and improvement of soil fertility. Some microorganisms can also cause diseases in plants, animals and humans.

Quick Facts to Remember

  • Microorganisms → organisms too small to be seen with the naked eye.
  • Microscope → helps us observe the microscopic world.
  • Amoeba and Paramecium → examples of protozoa.

  • Lactobacillus → helps in curd formation and food fermentation.
  • Decomposition → breakdown of complex waste into simpler substances.
  • Root-nodule microorganisms → help increase soil fertility by trapping nitrogen from air.
  • Spirulina → a protein-rich microalga discussed as a food resource.
  • Viruses → microscopic and acellular; multiply inside living cells.
  • Cell → basic unit of life.
  • Bacterial cell → lacks a well-defined nucleus and contains a nucleoid.

Examination Strategy

Exam Tips

  • Learn the definition of microorganisms and remember that they cannot be seen with the naked eye.
  • Be prepared to name the major groups discussed in the chapter: protozoa, algae, fungi and bacteria.
  • Clearly understand the difference between unicellular and multicellular organisms, with suitable examples.
  • Remember that yeast is a unicellular fungus and understand its role in fermentation.
  • For questions on baking, remember that yeast releases carbon dioxide, which makes dough soft and fluffy.
  • Remember the role of Lactobacillus in curd formation and food fermentation.
  • Understand decomposition and explain how microorganisms break down plant and animal waste into simpler, nutrient-rich substances.
  • Remember how microorganisms help in nutrient recycling and increasing soil fertility.
  • Learn the importance of microorganisms present in the root nodules of leguminous plants.
  • Remember that viruses are microscopic and acellular and multiply inside living cells.
  • For cell-based questions, remember that the cell is the basic unit of life and that bacterial cells contain a nucleoid instead of a well-defined nucleus.
  • Questions may ask you to distinguish between helpful and harmful microorganisms, so prepare examples of both.

Exam Focus: Pay special attention to definitions, examples, differences between types of microorganisms, the role of yeast and Lactobacillus, decomposition, soil fertility, and the special features of viruses and bacterial cells.
