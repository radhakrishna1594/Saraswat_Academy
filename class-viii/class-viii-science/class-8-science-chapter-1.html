Chapter Guide

Chapter Overview

Science begins with curiosity. Instead of simply learning facts, we learn how to find and understand facts through careful observation and simple experiments. The chapter explains that scientific investigation involves asking focused questions, identifying what can be changed or controlled, observing or measuring the results, and using the observations to improve our understanding.

The chapter gives examples from everyday life, such as the puffing of a puri, to show how a simple observation can become a scientific investigation. It highlights the importance of changing one factor at a time, keeping other conditions the same, recording observations, and asking new questions based on the results.

Overall, the chapter develops the habit of systematic investigation. It reminds us that science is not limited to laboratories—our surroundings and even our kitchen can become places for scientific discovery when we observe carefully, ask meaningful questions and investigate logically.

Key Ideas

  • Science begins with curiosity and questions.
  • Careful observation is an important part of scientific investigation.
  • Simple experiments can help us answer scientific questions.
  • Variables can be changed or controlled during an experiment.
  • Measurements and observations help us understand results.
  • It is useful to change only one factor at a time while keeping other conditions the same.
  • Recording observations helps in analysing an experiment.
  • Experiments can lead to new questions and further investigations.
  • Systematic investigation is an important way of thinking like a scientist.
Learn Clearly

Important Concepts and notes

1. Curiosity in Science

Curiosity is the starting point of scientific investigation. Scientists observe things around them and ask questions such as Why?, How? and What happens if...?

Everyday events can become interesting scientific questions. For example, the chapter asks why one side of a puri is thinner than the other and why a puri puffs up when placed in hot oil.

2. Scientific Investigation

Scientific investigation is a systematic way of finding answers to questions. It involves asking a focused question, planning an investigation, making observations or measurements, and using the results to improve our understanding.

3. Observation

Observation means carefully noticing and recording what happens during an investigation. Observations may include things that we see, hear, smell or otherwise sense.

For example, while frying a puri, we may observe whether it puffs up, whether the oil splatters, or whether the oil produces smoke.

4. Measurement

Some observations can be expressed using numbers. For example, instead of simply observing whether a puri puffs up, we can measure the time taken for it to puff up in seconds.

5. Variables

During an experiment, some factors can be changed or controlled to study their effect. These factors are called variables.

In the puri investigation, possible factors include:

  • Thickness of the rolled dough
  • Size of the rolled dough
  • Type of flour used
  • Temperature of the oil
  • Way in which the dough is placed into the oil

6. Change One Factor at a Time

A fair investigation should preferably involve changing only one factor at a time while keeping the other conditions the same. This helps us understand whether the change we made actually affected the result.

Example: If we want to study the effect of oil temperature, the thickness and size of the dough should be kept the same and the puris should be placed in the oil in the same way.

7. Recording Observations

It is important to keep notes of everything observed during an experiment. Proper records help us compare results, identify patterns and think of further questions.

8. Experiments Can Lead to New Questions

Scientific investigation does not always end with one answer. The results of an experiment can lead to new questions.

For example, after investigating a puri, we may ask whether puris puff better when made from fresh dough or stored dough, or what happens if a hole is made in the puri before frying.

9. Systematic Investigation

Systematic investigation means carrying out scientific investigations in an organised and careful manner. It involves asking questions, controlling suitable factors, observing or measuring results, recording findings and using those findings to develop further understanding.

10. Science in Everyday Life

Science is not limited to laboratories. Simple investigations can be carried out by observing things around us, even in our own homes or kitchens. The chapter uses the example of a puri puffing up to show how an ordinary event can be investigated scientifically.

11. Important Scientific Ideas Introduced

The chapter also provides a preview of several scientific areas that will be explored further in the book:

  • Microorganisms: Tiny organisms, some helpful and some harmful.
  • Health and Infections: The role of nutritious food, exercise, medicines and vaccines in maintaining health and fighting infections.
  • Electric Current: Its heating and magnetic effects and their applications.
  • Force and Pressure: How forces can change the speed or direction of objects and how pressure affects air movement.
  • Particles and Materials: The idea that materials are made of tiny particles and the classification of substances into elements, compounds and mixtures.
  • Solutions: Mixtures such as sugar dissolved in tea.
  • Light: Reflection from mirrors and bending of light through lenses.
  • Moon and Its Phases: The changing illuminated part of the Moon as seen from Earth.
  • Ecosystems: Relationships between living organisms and their environment.
  • Earth and Climate: Earth's conditions that support life and the challenges caused by changes in climate.

Quick Revision

  • Curiosity → encourages us to ask questions.
  • Question → gives direction to an investigation.
  • Observation → helps us notice what happens.
  • Measurement → expresses some observations using numbers.
  • Variable → a factor that can be changed or controlled.
  • Experiment → helps investigate a question.
  • One factor at a time → makes comparison more meaningful.
  • Recording → helps analyse and compare observations.
  • New questions → can arise from experimental results.
  • Systematic investigation → a careful and organised approach to scientific discovery.

Key Message: Think like a scientist—be curious, ask questions, observe carefully, experiment thoughtfully, record what you find, and use your observations to explore further.

Examination Strategy

Exam Tips

  • Remember that curiosity and questioning are the starting points of scientific investigation.
  • Learn the meaning of important terms such as observation, measurement, variable and systematic investigation.
  • For questions about experiments, remember the rule: change one factor at a time and keep other conditions the same.
  • Be able to identify what can be changed or controlled and what can be observed or measured.
  • Remember the puri example as an application of scientific investigation in everyday life.
  • Understand why recording observations is important—it helps us compare results and develop new questions.
  • Prepare short answers explaining how an experiment can lead to further questions.
  • Remember that science is not limited to laboratories; everyday situations can also be investigated scientifically.
  • For long-answer questions, explain the process in order: Question → Experiment → Observation/Measurement → Recording → Further Investigation.

Quick Tip: In exam answers, use scientific keywords such as curiosity, observation, measurement, variables, experiment, recording and systematic investigation.
